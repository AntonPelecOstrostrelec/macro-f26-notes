Problem Set 1 — IS-LM (Closed Economy)

Explain what the IS curve represents and why it slopes downward.
The IS curve represents all combinations of income (Y) and the real interest rate (r) at which the goods market is in equilibrium — that is, where planned expenditure equals output. It slopes downward because a lower r raises investment I, which raises planned expenditure, which by the multiplier raises Y. So every lower r corresponds to a higher equilibrium Y.
Explain what the LM curve represents and why it slopes upward.
The LM curve represents all combinations of income (Y) and the real interest rate (r) at which the money market is in equilibrium — real money supply equals real money demand. It slopes upward because higher Y raises money demand (more transactions); with the real money supply M/P fixed, the interest rate r must rise to discourage additional money holding, restoring equilibrium.
What is the IS-LM model and what does it explain?
The IS-LM model is a short-run model that simultaneously determines output Y and the real interest rate r in a closed economy. It combines goods market equilibrium (IS curve) and money market equilibrium (LM curve) into a single framework. The equilibrium is where IS = LM — the unique (Y*, r*) pair that clears both markets at once.
What is the government expenditure multiplier and how is it calculated?
The government expenditure multiplier measures how much equilibrium output Y rises per unit increase in government spending G. It equals 1/(1 − MPC), where MPC is the marginal propensity to consume. The multiplier exceeds 1 because each unit of new government spending generates further rounds of consumption — the recipient spends MPC×ΔG, who spends MPC² × ΔG, and so on.
Explain "crowding out" in the IS-LM framework.
Crowding out is the reduction in private investment that occurs when government spending rises. Higher G shifts IS right, raising both Y and r. The rise in r raises the cost of borrowing, so private firms invest less. The net effect on Y is therefore smaller than the simple multiplier would predict — some of the fiscal boost is "crowded out" by the rise in r.
Explain the effect of expansionary monetary policy (↑M) on Y and r in IS-LM.
Expansionary monetary policy shifts the LM curve to the right. With more real money supply, the interest rate r must fall to clear the money market. The lower r raises investment I, which raises planned expenditure and Y. Net effect: Y rises, r falls. This is the standard transmission of monetary easing.
Explain the effect of contractionary fiscal policy (e.g., ↑T) in IS-LM.
Higher taxes reduce disposable income, which reduces consumption and planned expenditure. The IS curve shifts left. The new equilibrium has lower Y and lower r. The drop in r encourages some private investment recovery, but the overall effect is a contraction in output. This is a common policy tool used to reduce deficits or cool an overheating economy.

Problem Set 3 — Mundell-Fleming (Open Economy)

Explain the difference between fixed and floating exchange rate regimes.
Under a fixed exchange rate regime, the central bank commits to maintaining the exchange rate e at a constant level by buying or selling foreign reserves whenever market forces would push e off target. Money supply M becomes endogenous, sacrificing monetary policy independence. Under a floating regime, e is determined entirely by market forces, and the central bank can independently control M (or interest rates) — but e fluctuates with capital flows and trade balances.
Under floating exchange rates, what is the effect of an expansionary fiscal policy (↑G)?
The IS* curve shifts right, but LM* is vertical (Y is pinned by perfect capital mobility and the fixed real money supply). The fiscal expansion puts upward pressure on r, attracting capital inflows. The currency appreciates (e ↑), making exports more expensive and imports cheaper. Net exports fall by exactly the amount that cancels the fiscal boost. Result: Y unchanged, e appreciates. Fiscal policy is INEFFECTIVE under floating ER.
Under floating exchange rates, what is the effect of expansionary monetary policy (↑M)?
The LM* curve shifts right. Downward pressure on r causes capital to flow out, so the currency depreciates (e ↓). The depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports more expensive, raising net exports NX. Higher NX raises Y. Result: Y rises, e depreciates. Monetary policy is EFFECTIVE under floating — the boost reaches the real economy through the exchange rate channel.
Under fixed exchange rates, what is the effect of expansionary fiscal policy (↑G)?
The IS* curve shifts right. To prevent the currency from appreciating, the central bank must sell domestic currency and buy foreign reserves. This expands the money supply M, shifting LM* right alongside IS*. Both curves shift together, producing a new equilibrium at higher Y at the same fixed e. Result: Y rises (by the full fiscal multiplier), e remains fixed. Fiscal policy is EFFECTIVE under fixed ER.
Under fixed exchange rates, why is monetary policy ineffective?
An expansionary monetary policy would shift LM* right and depreciate the currency. To maintain the peg, the central bank must reverse course by buying back its own currency, which contracts the money supply back to its original level. The LM* shift is fully undone. The central bank has surrendered monetary control to maintain the exchange rate peg. Result: Y unchanged.
Explain how a tariff affects net exports, the IS or LM curve, the exchange rate, and output under a fixed exchange rate system.
A tariff makes imports more expensive, raising net exports NX at any given exchange rate. The IS* curve shifts right (at every e, Y is higher). Under a fixed ER, the central bank must prevent the resulting appreciation by selling domestic currency, expanding the money supply. The LM* curve shifts right alongside IS*. Result: Y rises, e fixed, NX rises in equilibrium. Caveat: this expansion of M may cause inflationary pressure, and trading partners may retaliate, reducing the net long-run benefit.
Why does an appreciation of the exchange rate reduce net exports?
An appreciation makes domestic goods relatively more expensive in foreign markets, so foreign customers buy fewer exports (X falls). At the same time, it makes foreign goods cheaper at home, so domestic customers buy more imports (IM rises). Net exports NX = X − IM thus fall on both sides. This is why the IS* curve in Mundell-Fleming slopes downward in (e, Y) space.
Explain the effect of a rise in the world interest rate r* on a small open economy.
Under perfect capital mobility, the domestic r must equal r*, so domestic r rises. Higher r reduces investment and consumption, lowering Y. Under floating ER, capital flows in seeking the higher world rate, causing the currency to appreciate, which further reduces NX and Y. Under fixed ER, the central bank must defend the peg by selling reserves, which contracts M and lowers Y through the same monetary tightening channel.

Problem Set 4 — Solow Growth Model

Is the steady-state level of capital always equal to the Golden Rule level? Explain why or why not.
No, not in general. The steady-state k* depends on the saving rate s (a choice made by households or policy). The Golden Rule kgold is determined by technology — it satisfies MPK = δ + n + g, the condition that maximises consumption per worker. They coincide only when s equals a specific Golden Rule saving rate sgold. If s < sgold, the country is under-saving and could raise long-run consumption by saving more. If s > sgold, the country is over-saving and could raise consumption immediately by saving less.
Discuss how differences in saving rates between two countries affect their steady-state outcomes.
A higher saving rate raises the steady-state capital per worker k* and steady-state output per worker y*. With more saving, more investment occurs at every k, shifting the saving curve sf(k) up; its intersection with the break-even line (δ+n+g)k moves to the right. The country reaches a higher steady-state level. However, the long-run growth RATE of output per worker is unchanged — it always equals g (technology growth). Higher saving raises the LEVEL, not the rate, of long-run growth.
Discuss how higher population growth rate (n) affects the Solow steady state.
Higher population growth n steepens the break-even line (δ+n+g)k — the economy needs more investment to equip the larger workforce. The intersection with sf(k) shifts leftward, lowering steady-state k* and y* (capital per worker, output per worker). Total output Y grows faster (at n+g instead of g), but per-capita output is permanently lower. This explains why economists worry about both Malthusian traps and demographic stagnation.
What is the role of technology growth (g) in the Solow model with technological progress?
Technology growth g is the only source of sustained long-run growth in output per worker. While saving and population can change the level of per-capita output, they cannot sustain its growth — diminishing returns to capital eventually prevent that. Only continuous technological improvements raise productivity year after year. This is why advanced economies prioritise R&D, education, and innovation — these are the only tools for raising long-term living standards.

Problem Set 5 — Consumption Theory + CPI Analysis

Compare Keynes's consumption function with Friedman's Permanent Income Hypothesis.
Keynes (1936) proposed that consumption depends on current disposable income: C = a + b·Yd, where b (MPC) is between 0 and 1. The intercept a captures autonomous consumption. Friedman (1957) argued instead that consumption depends on permanent income YP: C = α·YP. Households smooth consumption over their lifetime expected income — transitory shocks are mostly saved, not spent. The key implication: under PIH, temporary tax rebates barely affect C, while Keynes predicts a multiplier × rebate boost. Empirically, PIH explains the Kuznets puzzle better.
Compare Keynes's consumption function with Modigliani's Life-Cycle Hypothesis.
Keynes (1936) said consumption depends on current disposable income: C = a + b·Yd. Modigliani (1954) argued that consumption depends on lifetime resources, not current income. People save during their working years and dissave in retirement to smooth consumption over their entire life. The LCH formula is C = (W + R·Y)/T, where W is wealth, R is remaining working years, and T is total years of life remaining. The implication is hump-shaped saving over the life-cycle, and consumption that responds to wealth as much as income.
Explain the Kuznets puzzle and how PIH/LCH solve it.
Simon Kuznets observed empirically that the average propensity to consume (APC = C/Y) is roughly constant in long-run time-series data but falls as income rises in cross-section data. Keynes's theory cannot explain this — it predicts APC always falls with income. Friedman's PIH solves it: in cross-section, higher-income households have a larger share of transitory income, so their measured APC is biased downward. In time-series, growth is mostly permanent, so APC stays constant. This is strong evidence that consumption responds to permanent rather than transitory income.
Why does the inflation rate in the "Education" CPI category often remain stable?
Education prices in Denmark are largely set by the public sector. Tuition fees are regulated by the Ministry of Education on annual or longer cycles, not adjusted month by month. Teacher salaries are set in collective bargaining agreements that lock in increases for multi-year periods. The result is that "Education" CPI moves only at programmed intervals, not in response to monthly market shocks. The flat value typically reflects a scheduled annual fee adjustment built into the system.
Why does the inflation rate in the "Restaurants and Hotels" CPI category show large variations?
Restaurants and hotels are highly sensitive to short-term factors. The sector is labour-intensive, so wage shocks transmit quickly. Demand is seasonal (driven by holidays and tourism), creating volatility. Energy and food input cost shocks flow through into menu prices. Finally, demand is highly elastic — when budgets tighten, consumers cut discretionary dining first, forcing rapid price adjustments. All these factors combine to make this category one of the most volatile in CPI.
How might inflation in essential goods (e.g., housing, energy) affect aggregate consumption if wages don't keep pace?
If essential goods inflation outpaces wage growth, real disposable income falls. Households cannot reduce essentials much (housing, utilities are inelastic), so they cut discretionary spending — restaurants, entertainment, clothing. Real consumption C falls. From the national income identity Y = C + I + G + NX, lower C reduces aggregate demand and Y. The drag may be amplified if the central bank tightens monetary policy to fight inflation, further reducing I. The burden falls disproportionately on low-income households, since essentials are a larger share of their budgets.

Concept Definitions (T/F + Short Answer)

Differentiate between frictional and structural unemployment.
Frictional unemployment arises from the time it takes for workers and jobs to find each other — job search, geographical moves, and consideration of offers. It is unavoidable in a dynamic economy but can be reduced through better job-matching information. Structural unemployment arises when wages are set above the market-clearing level — through minimum wage laws, union bargaining, or efficiency wages — creating a persistent gap between labour supply and demand. Together they form the natural rate of unemployment un.
Explain the Quantity Theory of Money and how it determines the inflation rate.
The Quantity Theory of Money states that M × V = P × Y, where M is the money supply, V is the velocity of money, P is the price level, and Y is real output. In growth-rate form: %ΔM + %ΔV ≈ %ΔP + %ΔY. With V approximately constant in the long run, inflation equals the money growth rate minus the real output growth rate: %ΔP ≈ %ΔM − %ΔY. So if M grows at 8% and real Y grows at 2%, inflation will be approximately 6%.
Explain money neutrality and the classical dichotomy.
Money neutrality is the principle that in the long run, changes in the money supply M affect only nominal variables (prices, nominal wages, nominal interest rates) — not real variables (output Y, employment, real interest rates, or real wages). The classical dichotomy separates these two sets of variables: real variables are determined by real factors (capital, labour, technology), while nominal variables are determined by monetary factors. This is why simply printing more money cannot make a country genuinely richer in the long run.
Explain what stagflation is and how it arises.
Stagflation refers to the simultaneous occurrence of stagnant output (falling Y, rising unemployment) and high inflation (rising P). It typically arises from an adverse supply shock — an oil price spike, war, pandemic — which raises input costs across the economy. The SRAS curve shifts up: at every Y, firms must charge higher P. The new short-run equilibrium has both higher P AND lower Y. Stagflation creates a policy dilemma because the usual tools (monetary tightening fights inflation but worsens output; easing helps output but worsens inflation) cannot solve both problems simultaneously.
Describe the short-run Phillips curve and the long-run Phillips curve.
The short-run Phillips curve describes the trade-off between inflation and unemployment: π = πe − β(u − un) + ν. When unemployment u falls below the natural rate un, inflation rises above expectations. The trade-off is exploitable in the short run but disappears in the long run. The long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate un: there is no permanent trade-off between inflation and unemployment. Long-run inflation is determined by monetary policy alone, while u settles at un regardless.
State and explain Okun's law.
Okun's law states that a 1 percentage point increase in unemployment is associated with roughly a 2% decrease in real GDP relative to potential output. The relationship is negative — higher unemployment means lower output. Equivalently, more employment means higher output (positive relationship). This empirical regularity is useful for translating between labour-market conditions and aggregate output.
Explain Ricardian equivalence.
Ricardian equivalence (Robert Barro, 1974) is the theory that forward-looking households anticipate the future taxes implied by today's deficit and increase their savings accordingly. Private saving rises by exactly the amount public saving falls. National saving and the interest rate are unchanged, so deficits do not crowd out investment. In practice, Ricardian equivalence holds only partially — many households are not fully forward-looking, face borrowing constraints, or face uncertainty about future taxes.
Explain the time inconsistency problem in monetary policy.
Time inconsistency (Kydland and Prescott, 1977) refers to the central bank's incentive to deviate from announced policies once private agents have committed. If the CB announces low inflation and workers set wages accordingly, the CB then has an incentive to engineer surprise inflation to push output above potential. But workers anticipate this and demand higher wages from the start, leading to high inflation without any output gain. The solution is institutional commitment — making the CB independent and rule-based to make the low-inflation promise credible.
What is the Fisher equation and what does it tell us?
The Fisher equation states that the nominal interest rate equals the real interest rate plus expected inflation: i = r + πe. Rearranging: r = i − πe. This tells us that the "real return" on saving accounts for the erosion of purchasing power from inflation. When inflation rises, nominal rates tend to rise proportionally (the Fisher effect), leaving the real rate roughly unchanged in the long run.

"In Your Opinion" Question Structures

How to structure "in your opinion" answers. Don't just give an opinion — anchor in economic theory. Three paragraphs: (1) State the immediate effects on key variables (Y, r, employment, etc.). (2) Discuss the long-run effects via the relevant framework (Solow, AD-AS, Phillips). (3) Verdict with caveats based on the economy's current state. Below are templates.
In your opinion, how does a fiscal expansion (↑G) affect society?
A fiscal expansion has both benefits and costs. Short-run benefits: higher G raises planned expenditure and Y, reducing cyclical unemployment and supporting household incomes. If the economy is below potential, this is welfare-improving. Short-run costs: r rises (crowding out), so private investment falls. The fiscal multiplier is therefore smaller than the simple Keynesian prediction. Long-run costs: lower I means a smaller capital stock and lower potential output Y̅ for future generations; higher deficits increase public debt. The verdict depends on context: in recession, the gains likely exceed costs; at full employment, fiscal expansion risks inflation without sustainable real gains.
In your opinion, how does a contractionary fiscal policy (↑T) affect society?
Higher taxes reduce disposable income, lowering consumption and aggregate demand. The short-run effect is a fall in Y and rise in unemployment — a real cost in welfare and household incomes. However, there are long-run benefits: lower r (less crowding out) allows more private investment, which boosts long-run productive capacity; smaller deficits reduce public debt accumulation, supporting fiscal sustainability and reducing future tax burden. The trade-off is essentially "short-run pain for long-run gain." The verdict depends on the economy's state: when overheating with high inflation, contractionary fiscal makes sense; in a recession, it deepens the downturn and is generally not recommended.
In your opinion, how should the central bank respond to an adverse supply shock?
The central bank faces a painful dilemma. Expansionary monetary policy (↓r) would shift AD right, restoring Y back toward Y̅, but at the cost of higher inflation — accommodating the supply shock. Contractionary monetary policy (↑r) would fight inflation but deepen the recession. A third option is to wait for natural adjustment as wages and expectations adjust, but this is slow and prolongs unemployment. The best response is often supply-side intervention — subsidies for energy or inputs that directly counter the SRAS shift — though this is often expensive and slow. Historically, the Volcker Fed in 1979 chose tightening to break inflation expectations; this caused severe recession but ended the persistent inflation cycle.
