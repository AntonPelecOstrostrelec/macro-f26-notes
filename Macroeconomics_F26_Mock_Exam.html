Problem Set 1 — Keynesian Cross & IS-LM (Case Study) 30% · 72 min

Consider a hypothetical closed economy, Pratur-land, where the goods market is described by the following equations:

PE (Planned Expenditure) = C + I + G
where C = 100 + 0.75(Y − T),   I = 300,   T = 200,   G = 300.
AE (Actual Expenditure) = Y.

(a) Using the Keynesian cross approach, determine the equilibrium national income Y, consumption C, private saving Spriv, public saving Spub, and identify the value of the government expenditure multiplier.

Equilibrium Y — step by step:
Step 1. Start with the equilibrium condition Y = PE = C + I + G.
Step 2. Substitute the given functions and values.
  Y = [100 + 0.75(Y − T)] + I + G
  Y = [100 + 0.75(Y − 200)] + 300 + 300
Step 3. Distribute the 0.75 across (Y − 200): 0.75·Y − 0.75·200 = 0.75Y − 150.
  Y = 100 + 0.75Y − 150 + 300 + 300
Step 4. Combine constant terms: 100 − 150 + 300 + 300 = 550.
  Y = 0.75Y + 550
Step 5. Move 0.75Y to the left side (subtract 0.75Y from both sides).
  Y − 0.75Y = 550
  0.25Y = 550
Step 6. Divide both sides by 0.25.
  Y = 550 ÷ 0.25 = 2,200
Consumption:
C = 100 + 0.75(2,200 − 200) = 100 + 1,500 = 1,600
Private saving:
Spriv = Y − T − C = 2,200 − 200 − 1,600 = 400
Public saving:
Spub = T − G = 200 − 300 = −100 (deficit)
National saving check: S = Spriv + Spub = 400 − 100 = 300 = I ✓ Government expenditure multiplier:
Multiplier = 1/(1 − MPC) = 1/(1 − 0.75) = 4
Meaning: a 100-unit rise in G would raise Y by 400 in the pure Keynesian cross (before interest-rate adjustment).

Now suppose investment is no longer fixed but depends on the interest rate (r), as represented by:

I = 500 − 50r

(b) Derive the IS equation using the given information.

Step 1. Start from Y = C + I(r) + G — same as before but I now depends on r.
Step 2. Substitute: Y = 100 + 0.75(Y − 200) + (500 − 50r) + 300
Step 3. Distribute 0.75 across (Y − 200): Y = 100 + 0.75Y − 150 + 500 − 50r + 300
Step 4. Combine constants: 100 − 150 + 500 + 300 = 750
  Y = 0.75Y + 750 − 50r
Step 5. Move 0.75Y to the left: Y − 0.75Y = 750 − 50r → 0.25Y = 750 − 50r
Step 6. Divide by 0.25 (multiply by 4): Y = 4·750 − 4·50r = 3,000 − 200r
  IS: Y = 3,000 − 200r

(c) Explain in 2–3 sentences what the IS curve represents and why it slopes downward.

Model answer: "The IS curve represents all combinations of income Y and the real interest rate r at which the goods market is in equilibrium — that is, where planned expenditure equals output. It slopes downward because a lower r raises investment I, which raises planned expenditure, which by the multiplier raises Y. So every lower r corresponds to a higher equilibrium Y."

Additionally, the money market is represented by:

Real money demand: (M/P)d = Y − 50r
Nominal money supply M = 4,000,   price level P = 4.

(d) Derive the LM equation.

M/P = 4,000 / 4 = 1,000
Set demand = supply: 1,000 = Y − 50r
LM: Y = 1,000 + 50r

(e) Explain in 2–3 sentences what the LM curve represents and why it slopes upward.

Model answer: "The LM curve represents all combinations of (Y, r) at which the money market is in equilibrium — real money supply equals real money demand. It slopes upward because higher Y raises money demand (more transactions); with the real money supply M/P fixed, the interest rate r must rise to discourage additional money holding, restoring equilibrium."

(f) Using the IS-LM framework, determine the equilibrium level of income Y* and interest rate r*.

Step 1. Set IS equal to LM (both must give the same Y at equilibrium):
  3,000 − 200r = 1,000 + 50r
Step 2. Move constants to the left, r terms to the right (add 200r to both sides, subtract 1,000 from both sides):
  3,000 − 1,000 = 50r + 200r
  2,000 = 250r
Step 3. Divide both sides by 250:
  r* = 2,000 ÷ 250 = 8 (i.e. 8%)
Step 4. Substitute r* = 8 back into the LM equation to find Y*:
  Y* = 1,000 + 50(8) = 1,000 + 400 = 1,400
Step 5. Sanity check: plug r* = 8 into IS too:
  Y = 3,000 − 200(8) = 3,000 − 1,600 = 1,400 ✓ Both equations give Y = 1,400. Correct.

(g) Suppose the government implements an expansionary fiscal policy by raising G from 300 to 400 (with T unchanged). Calculate the new equilibrium Y and r using the IS-LM framework.

New IS (with G = 400):
Y = 100 + 0.75(Y − 200) + (500 − 50r) + 400
Y = 100 + 0.75Y − 150 + 500 − 50r + 400
0.25Y = 850 − 50r
IS': Y = 3,400 − 200r
Solve IS' = LM:
3,400 − 200r = 1,000 + 50r
2,400 = 250r → r* = 9.6 (9.6%)
Y* = 1,000 + 50(9.6) = 1,480
So Y rises by 80 (from 1,400 to 1,480) and r rises by 1.6 percentage points (from 8% to 9.6%).
Crowding out: the simple multiplier would predict ΔY = 4 × 100 = 400. The actual ΔY is only 80 — the rest is crowded out by the rise in r reducing investment.

(h) In your opinion, how does this fiscal expansion affect society as a whole? Answer using economic logic.

Model A-grade answer:
"The fiscal expansion has both benefits and costs that must be weighed against each other.

Benefits: Y rises from 1,400 to 1,480, which raises employment and short-term welfare. If the economy is in a recession (Y below potential), this is a clear positive — reducing cyclical unemployment, raising household incomes, and lifting aggregate demand. Government spending also funds public goods (infrastructure, education) that provide additional long-term benefits not captured by GDP alone.

Costs: The fiscal multiplier is heavily dampened by crowding out — only 80 of the expected 400 ΔY materialises because r rises from 8% to 9.6%, reducing private investment I. Lower I means slower capital accumulation, reducing long-run Y̅ (Solow logic). The deficit also widens (Spub falls further), increasing public debt that future generations must service.

Verdict: Whether the policy is wise depends on context. If the economy is below potential and there's slack in capital markets, the gains likely outweigh the costs. If the economy is at potential, this is likely to fuel inflation and crowd out productive private investment — a poor trade-off. Context determines verdict."

Problem Set 2 — True/False 10% · 20 min

State whether each statement is True or False and give one sentence of reasoning.

(a) Doubling all inputs in a production technology raises output from 60 to 110 units. This is increasing returns to scale (IRS).

FALSE
Output rose by only 83% (110/60 ≈ 1.83 < 2). Output less than doubled, so this is decreasing returns to scale (DRS), not IRS.

(b) Under perfect capital mobility and fixed exchange rates, expansionary monetary policy effectively raises domestic output.

FALSE
Under fixed ER, monetary policy is INEFFECTIVE. ↑M would depreciate the currency; the central bank must buy back the currency to defend the peg, undoing ΔM. Floating gives monetary, fixed gives fiscal.

(c) According to the Quantity Theory of Money, an increase in the money supply raises real output (Y) in the long run.

FALSE
QTM with the classical dichotomy says money is neutral in the long run: ΔM affects only P (proportionally), not real Y, real r, or employment. Real output is determined by K, L, A — not by M.

(d) The short-run Phillips curve shows a positive relationship between unemployment and inflation.

FALSE
The short-run Phillips curve shows a NEGATIVE trade-off: higher u → lower π (formula π = πe − β(u − un)). The relationship reverses in the long run, which is vertical at un (no permanent trade-off).

(e) The marginal propensity to consume (MPC) measures the change in consumption per unit change in disposable income and always satisfies 0 < MPC < 1.

TRUE
MPC = ∂C/∂Yd. Standard Keynesian assumption: out of every extra unit of disposable income, people spend a fraction (between 0 and 1) on consumption and save the rest.

Problem Set 3 — Open Economy / Mundell-Fleming 20% · 48 min

Consider two economies, Denmark and Sweden. Denmark maintains a fixed exchange rate (pegged to the euro), while Sweden operates under a floating exchange rate (SEK floats against the EUR and USD).

(a) Explain the key difference between fixed and floating exchange rate regimes, and what this means for monetary policy independence.

Model answer:
"Under a fixed exchange rate regime, the central bank commits to keeping the exchange rate e constant against another currency. To do this, it buys/sells its own currency in exchange for foreign reserves whenever market forces would push e off target. As a result, the money supply M becomes endogenous — the CB cannot independently set M, because doing so would force a deviation from the peg. Monetary policy is therefore not independent.

Under a floating exchange rate regime, the exchange rate is set entirely by market forces (supply and demand for the currency). The central bank can independently set M (or the policy interest rate), and the exchange rate adjusts in response. Monetary policy is fully independent.

Trade-off: Fixed regime gives currency stability (good for trade-heavy economies like Denmark) but sacrifices monetary autonomy. Floating regime gives monetary autonomy but accepts exchange-rate volatility."

(b) Sweden's central bank cuts the policy interest rate. Using the Mundell-Fleming model, explain which curve shifts, and the effect on Swedish output and the krona's exchange rate.

Model answer:
"The cut in r is equivalent to expansionary monetary policy (↑M). The LM* curve shifts right (in (e, Y) space). The vertical LM* line was at one Y; the new LM*' is at a higher Y.

Mechanism:
1. ↑M creates downward pressure on r.
2. But r must equal r* (world rate). So capital flows OUT of Sweden — investors leave for better returns abroad.
3. Selling SEK to buy foreign assets → krona DEPRECIATES (e falls).
4. Cheaper krona makes Swedish exports cheap abroad and imports expensive → NX rises.
5. Higher NX raises Y.

Result: Y rises, krona depreciates. Monetary policy is EFFECTIVE under floating. The transmission works through the exchange rate, not directly through r."

(c) Denmark introduces import tariffs on certain goods (a trade-policy intervention). Explain how this affects Denmark's net exports NX, the IS or LM curve, the exchange rate, and output under the fixed-ER regime.

Model answer:
"Tariffs make imports more expensive, raising net exports NX at any given exchange rate. The IS* curve shifts right (more spending on domestic goods at every e).

Mechanism under fixed ER:
1. IS* shifts right → upward pressure on e (DKK would appreciate without intervention).
2. To defend the peg, Nationalbanken sells DKK for EUR reserves. This expands the money supply M.
3. ↑M → LM* shifts right alongside IS*.
4. Both curves shift, new equilibrium at higher Y, same e.

Result: Y rises, e stays fixed, NX rises in equilibrium, M expands.

Caveats: The expansion of M may cause inflation pressure later. Also, tariffs typically invite retaliation from trading partners, which could erase the NX gain. From a pure welfare standpoint, tariffs distort trade and create deadweight losses — short-run Y gain comes at long-run efficiency costs."

(d) Suppose Sweden's interest rate rises (driven by world rates rising, so r* rises). Using the aggregate demand identity Y = C + I + G + NX, with C = f(Yd) and I = f(r), analyse the impact on investment I, consumption C, and output Y.

Model answer:
"Direct effect on I: Higher r raises the cost of capital (r + δ). Fewer projects clear the MPK > r + δ threshold. Investment I falls.

Indirect effect on C: Lower I reduces aggregate demand, lowering Y. Lower Y means lower disposable income (Yd = Y − T). Lower Yd reduces consumption C (via C = a + b·Yd, with MPC = b).

Net effect on Y: Both I and C fall, so Y falls significantly. The combined effect is amplified by the multiplier — the initial fall in I gets magnified by 1/(1−MPC) before the new equilibrium is reached.

NX effect (open-economy side): Higher r* attracts global capital. SEK appreciates. This reduces NX, further dampening Y.

Bottom line: Y falls through three channels: ↓I, ↓C, and ↓NX. G is fixed by policy and remains unchanged."

Problem Set 4 — Solow Growth Model 20% · 48 min

Consider two countries A and B that have the same depreciation rate but differ in saving rates and population/technology growth rates. The following information is provided:

Production function (per worker) for both countries: y = f(k) = k1/2
Saving function (per worker):
  Country A: sf(k) = 0.25·k1/2   (so s = 0.25)
  Country B: sf(k) = 0.40·k1/2   (so s = 0.40)
Depreciation rate δ = 0.04 in both countries.
Population growth: n = 0.02 in both countries.
Technology growth: gA = 0.04 in Country A, gB = 0.02 in Country B.
Fundamental equation: Δk = sf(k) − (δ + n + g)k

(a) Calculate the steady-state levels of capital stock k*, output per worker y*, saving per worker, and consumption per worker c* for both Country A and Country B.

Country A — step by step:
Step 1. Compute the denominator δ + n + g (the "break-even rate"):
  δ + n + g = 0.04 + 0.02 + 0.04 = 0.10
Step 2. Apply the steady-state formula k* = (s / (δ+n+g))² (derived from sf(k*) = (δ+n+g)·k*):
  k*A = (0.25 / 0.10)² = (2.5)² = 6.25
Step 3. Compute output per worker y* = √k* (since y = k1/2):
  y*A = √6.25 = 2.5
Step 4. Saving per worker = s × y*:
  sy*A = 0.25 × 2.5 = 0.625
Step 5. Consumption per worker c* = (1 − s) × y*:
  c*A = 0.75 × 2.5 = 1.875
Sanity check: sy* + c* should equal y*. 0.625 + 1.875 = 2.5 ✓
Country B — step by step:
Step 1. δ + n + g = 0.04 + 0.02 + 0.02 = 0.08
Step 2. k*B = (0.40 / 0.08)² = (5)² = 25
Step 3. y*B = √25 = 5
Step 4. sy*B = 0.40 × 5 = 2
Step 5. c*B = (1 − 0.40) × 5 = 0.60 × 5 = 3
Sanity check: 2 + 3 = 5 = y* ✓
Comparison: Country B has 4× higher k*, 2× higher y*, and 60% higher consumption per worker than Country A.

(b) Discuss how the differences in saving rates and technology growth rates between Country A and Country B impact their steady-state outcomes. Which factor matters more for the level of output per worker?

Model answer:
"Country B has both a higher saving rate (0.40 vs 0.25) AND a lower (δ+n+g) denominator (0.08 vs 0.10 — because its technology growth is lower). Both effects push Country B to higher steady-state k* and y*.

Why higher s raises levels: Higher saving means more investment per worker at every k, so the sf(k) curve shifts up. The intersection with the break-even line moves rightward, giving higher k* and y*.

Why lower g raises level (but lowers growth rate): Lower technology growth means less 'capital dilution' from new technology — the break-even line is shallower, so steady-state k* is higher. But lower g means a lower long-run growth rate of y per worker (which equals g).

Which matters more for levels? Mathematically, k* = (s/(δ+n+g))². Both s and (δ+n+g) appear inside, but s is squared in the numerator. So a doubling of s (from 0.25 to 0.5, say) quadruples k*. The same proportional change in (δ+n+g) has the same impact in the opposite direction. Both matter.

Important caveat: Levels are not growth rates. Both countries grow per-worker at their g rate in the long run. Country A grows at g=4%; Country B at g=2%. So Country A's y will eventually CATCH UP and OVERTAKE Country B in the very long run — though it starts from a much lower level."

(c) In the context of the Solow growth model, is the steady-state level of capital stock always equal to the Golden Rule level? Explain your answer.

Model answer:
"No, not in general. The steady-state k* depends on the saving rate s (a choice made by households or policy). The Golden Rule kgold is determined by technology — specifically, kgold is the level of k that satisfies MPK = δ + n + g (i.e., the marginal product of capital equals the break-even investment rate).

They coincide only when s equals a specific Golden Rule saving rate sgold.

Three cases:
1. s < sgold (under-saving): The country has too little capital. Raising s would push k* toward kgold, eventually increasing consumption per worker. Most countries are believed to be in this case.
2. s = sgold (Golden Rule): The country maximises long-run c*. Hard to achieve in practice.
3. s > sgold (over-saving): The country has too much capital — consumption is being sacrificed unnecessarily. Lowering s would increase c* immediately.

For our two countries: We'd need to check whether their s values are above or below sgold. With Country A's s=0.25 and B's s=0.40, B is closer to typical Golden Rule values (around 30–50% depending on assumptions). Country A is likely under-saving."

Problem Set 5 — Consumption Theory + Danish CPI Analysis 20% · 48 min

(a) Explain how Friedman's Permanent Income Hypothesis (PIH) emphasises the role of permanent income over transitory income in shaping consumption decisions. Compare this with Keynes's consumption function. Use the Kuznets puzzle in your discussion.

Model answer (3-paragraph A-grade structure):

Paragraph 1 — Keynes (1936) Absolute Income Hypothesis:
"Keynes proposed that consumption depends primarily on current disposable income, captured by C = a + b·Yd, where b is the marginal propensity to consume (0 < b < 1). The intercept a represents autonomous consumption. The average propensity to consume (APC = C/Y = a/Y + b) falls as income rises, because the autonomous component a becomes a smaller share of total income. This is consistent with the observation that richer households save proportionally more.

Paragraph 2 — Friedman (1957) Permanent Income Hypothesis:
"Friedman challenged Keynes by arguing that consumption depends on permanent income YP, not current income. C = α·YP, where α is a constant fraction. Permanent income is the long-run expected income, smoothed across many years. Households smooth consumption based on this permanent income, mostly ignoring transitory shocks. A windfall (lottery win, unexpected bonus) is mostly saved. An unexpected loss is mostly absorbed by reducing saving rather than cutting C significantly.

Paragraph 3 — The Kuznets puzzle and how PIH solves it:
"Simon Kuznets observed empirically that APC is roughly constant in long-run time-series data, but falls as income rises in cross-section data. Keynes's theory cannot explain this — it predicts APC always falls with income. PIH solves the puzzle: in cross-section, higher-income households have a larger share of transitory income (lucky year), so their measured APC of permanent income is biased downward. In long-run time-series, growth is mostly permanent, so APC stays constant. This is strong evidence for PIH over Keynes for understanding long-run consumption behaviour. Policy implication: temporary tax rebates have little effect on C under PIH, while Keynes would predict a full MPC × rebate boost to C — empirical evidence largely supports PIH for predictable, large changes."

Model answer:

• Housing: 4.2 − 1.9 = 2.3 pp

1. It is labour-intensive, so wage shocks transmit quickly.

(c) In your opinion, why did the inflation rate in the "10: Education" category remain unchanged across all four months?

Model answer:
"Education prices in Denmark are largely set by the public sector. Tuition fees are regulated by the Ministry of Education on annual or longer cycles, not adjusted month-by-month. Salaries of teachers and staff are set in collective bargaining agreements that lock in increases for multi-year periods. The result is that 'Education' CPI moves only at policy-set intervals, not in response to monthly market shocks. The constant 2.1% likely reflects a programmed annual fee adjustment built into the system. This contrasts with market-driven categories (food, restaurants) where supply, demand, and input cost shocks transmit to prices much faster."

(d) Suppose inflation rises sharply in essential goods like "04: Housing, water, electricity, gas, fuels" but wages do not increase at the same rate. Using the national income identity Y = C + I + G, explain how this might influence overall consumption patterns and aggregate output.

Model answer:
"When inflation in essentials rises but wages don't follow, real disposable income falls (Yd in real terms = nominal Yd / P, declining as P rises). Households face a difficult re-budgeting problem.

Effect on C: Essentials (housing, utilities) have inelastic demand — people don't significantly reduce their consumption of them when prices rise. So spending on essentials rises in nominal terms (same quantity, higher prices). To balance the budget, households cut spending on discretionary goods (entertainment, restaurants, clothing). Total nominal C may be roughly unchanged, but real C falls. Many lower-income households also borrow or dip into savings.

Effect on Y: Y = C + I + G + NX (open economy). Real C falls → real Y falls. The drop in C also reduces business revenue in non-essential sectors → firms may cut investment I and lay off workers, further reducing Y (a contractionary multiplier).

Policy response: The central bank may tighten (↑r) to bring inflation down, which lowers I further. Alternatively, the government may use targeted subsidies (housing or energy support) to soften the impact on households without compounding inflation. Both responses involve trade-offs.

Distributional impact: The burden falls disproportionately on low-income households (essentials are a larger share of their budgets), so this kind of inflation shock tends to worsen inequality and reduce overall welfare even if measured Y holds up nominally."

Bonus Drill — Extra T/F Practice No time limit, for warm-up

If you finish the mock with time to spare, drill these extra T/F statements to sharpen reflexes. Cover the answers first, decide T/F, then check.

StatementAnswer + reasoning
1. Doubling all inputs raises output from 80 to 200. This is constant returns to scale. FALSE — 200/80 = 2.5 > 2, so this is IRS, not CRS.
2. The IS curve shows combinations of (Y, r) where the money market is in equilibrium. FALSE — IS represents the GOODS market equilibrium. LM is the money market.
3. Under floating exchange rates, fiscal expansion fully raises output. FALSE — Under floating, fiscal is INEFFECTIVE. IS* shifts but Y is pinned by vertical LM*; currency appreciates and NX falls to cancel the boost.
4. Nominal GDP is calculated at constant base-year prices. FALSE — Reversed. Nominal GDP is at CURRENT prices; Real GDP is at constant base-year prices.
5. According to Okun's law, a 1% increase in unemployment is associated with roughly a 2% increase in GDP relative to potential. FALSE — Sign reversed. A 1 pp increase in u is associated with a roughly 2% DECREASE in GDP relative to potential.
6. In the Solow model with technological progress, the long-run growth rate of output per worker equals g (technology growth rate). TRUE — Only technology growth sustains per-capita growth long-run. Capital accumulation alone hits diminishing returns.
7. CPI uses fixed quantity weights (Laspeyres), while the GDP deflator uses current quantity weights (Paasche). TRUE — Standard. The exam often tests this by reversing the definitions; watch carefully.
8. The Phillips curve trade-off between inflation and unemployment is permanent in the long run. FALSE — In the long run, the Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate un. No permanent trade-off.
9. According to the classical model, factors of production are paid their marginal products. TRUE — Real wage W/P = MPL; real rental rate R/P = MPK. Standard classical theory.
10. According to Friedman's PIH, a temporary tax cut produces a large boost in consumption. FALSE — Under PIH, temporary (transitory) income shocks barely affect C. Households mostly save the windfall. Keynes would predict a multiplier effect.
11. The Quantity Theory of Money says inflation equals money growth rate minus real output growth rate (when velocity is constant). TRUE — From M·V = P·Y in growth-rate form: %ΔP = %ΔM − %ΔY when %ΔV = 0.
12. The natural rate of unemployment equals zero in an idealised classical economy. FALSE — un = frictional + structural unemployment. Always positive, even in the best economy, because job-matching takes time.
13. The Fisher equation states that the real interest rate equals the nominal rate plus expected inflation. FALSE — Reversed. r = i − πe. Real = nominal MINUS expected inflation.
14. In the IS-LM model, an increase in the money supply shifts the LM curve to the right and reduces the interest rate. TRUE — ↑M → real money supply M/P rises (P sticky short-run) → LM shifts right → at any Y, r falls.
15. The Cobb-Douglas function Y = KαL(1−α) with α = 0.3 means labour earns 30% of national income. FALSE — Labour earns its exponent (1−α) = 70%, capital earns α = 30%. Reverse the indexing.

Final Exam-Day Tactical Tips

Time allocation (4 hours, 240 min): 72 min PS1 (30%), 20 min PS2 T/F (10%), 48 min PS3 (20%), 48 min PS4 (20%), 48 min PS5 (20%), 4 min review. Stick to it — don't let one section consume your whole exam.
Always show working. Even if your final answer is wrong, you get partial credit for correct steps. Make it easy for the grader to track your logic.
For "in your opinion" questions: always anchor in economic theory. Don't just give an opinion — use the framework (Y = C + I + G, IS-LM, Phillips curve, etc.) and arrive at a reasoned verdict, even one with caveats.
For T/F: Answer first ("FALSE."), then ONE sentence of reasoning. No more, no less. Read each statement twice — they love reversed definitions, swapped signs, and "always/never" overstatements.
Sketch diagrams when relevant. Always label axes (Y horizontal; r/e/P vertical). Show curve shifts with arrows. A clean diagram earns marks; a messy one loses them.
Sanity-check your numbers. If you compute MPC > 1, or a multiplier < 0, or negative Y, you've made an error. Catch these before moving on.
Don't panic on a confusing question. Move on, finish what you know, then come back. There's always partial credit available even for incomplete answers.
