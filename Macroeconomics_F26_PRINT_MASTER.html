Part A — 1-Page Cheat Sheet

National Income Identity & Multipliers

Y = C + I + G (closed) | Y = C + I + G + NX (open)
C = a + b·Yd | Yd = Y − T | b = MPC ∈ (0,1)
Spriv = Y − T − C | Spub = T − G | S = Spriv + Spub
Gov multiplier = 1/(1−MPC) | Tax mult = −MPC/(1−MPC) | Balanced budget = 1
Open multiplier = 1/(1 − MPC + m)

IS-LM Model (closed)

IS: Y = C(Y−T) + I(r) + G → slopes DOWN
LM: M/P = L(Y, r) typically Y − cr → slopes UP
↑G or ↓T: IS right → Y↑, r↑ (crowding out: I↓)
↑M: LM right → Y↑, r↓

Mundell-Fleming

r = r* (perfect capital mobility) | ↑e → ↓NX
LM* is VERTICAL; IS* slopes down in (e,Y)
FloatingFixed
Fiscal (↑G)INEFFECTIVE (e↑)EFFECTIVE (M↑)
Monetary (↑M)EFFECTIVE (e↓)INEFFECTIVE
Tariff (↑NX)e↑, Y sameY↑, e fixed

Memory hook: "Floating gives monetary, fixed gives fiscal."

Classical Production

Cobb-Douglas: Y = A·Kα·Lβ. α+β=1 → CRS; >1 IRS; <1 DRS.
RTS test: doubling raises Y by ratio R: R>2 IRS, =2 CRS, <2 DRS
MPL = (1−α)·Y/L | MPK = α·Y/K
W/P = MPL | R/P = MPK
Labour income = (1−α)·Y | Capital income = α·Y

Money & Fisher

QTM: M·V = P·Y → %ΔP ≈ %ΔM − %ΔY (V, Y fixed) = money neutrality
Fisher: i = r + πe → r = i − πe

Solow (square-root case)

y = k1/2 | Δk = sk1/2 − (δ+n+g)k
Steady state: k* = [s/(δ+n+g)]² | y* = √k* | sy* = s·y* | c* = (1−s)·y*
Golden Rule: MPK = δ+n+g (maximises c*)
Long-run per-capita growth = g (technology only)

Inflation, CPI, Phillips, Okun

CPI = fixed weights (Laspeyres) | GDP deflator = current quantities (Paasche)
π = (Pt − Pt−1)/Pt−1 · 100
Phillips: π = πe − β(u − un) + ν
Okun: ΔY/Y ≈ −β·(u − un); u↑ ↔ Y↓; employment↑ ↔ Y↑

Consumption Theories

Keynes: C = a + b·Yd → APC = a/Y + b falls as Y rises
Friedman PIH: C = α·YP (permanent income smoothing)
Modigliani LCH: C = (W + R·Y)/T (lifetime smoothing)
Kuznets puzzle solved by PIH/LCH (APC constant long-run, falls cross-section)

Part B — Variable Effects Reference

When asked "what happens when X changes?" — find X here, copy the chain + plain-English explanation, adapt to the question.

Goods market

↑G (Government spending rises)

↑G (government spending) → ↑PE (planned expenditure = C+I+G) → ↑Y (output, via multiplier 1/(1−MPC)) → ↑money demand → ↑r (real interest rate) → ↓I (investment — this is crowding out)
In plain English. Government spends more → demand for goods rises directly → firms produce more, so Y goes up. But more activity means people need more cash, pushing interest rates up. Higher rates make borrowing more expensive, so private investment falls — partly cancelling the boost. This is crowding out.

↑T (Taxes rise)

↑T (taxes) → ↓Yd (disposable income = Y−T) → ↓C (consumption) → ↓PE → ↓Y → ↓r → ↑I partially offsets
In plain English. Taxes rise → households have less to spend → consumption falls → total demand drops → Y falls. Smaller effect than an equal cut in G because households save part of the tax change anyway.

↑MPC (Marginal propensity to consume rises)

↑MPC → larger multiplier (1/(1−MPC)) → any ΔG or Δa has bigger effect on Y
In plain English. People spend a bigger fraction of any extra income → each policy shock gets amplified more rounds before settling. Fiscal policy becomes more powerful but the economy becomes more volatile.

Money market

↑M (Money supply rises)

↑M (money supply) → ↑(M/P) → ↓r (real interest rate falls) → ↑I → ↑PE → ↑Y
In plain English. Central bank prints more money → more cash chasing same investment → interest rates fall → cheaper to borrow → firms invest more → Y rises. Long-run: prices rise proportionally, real economy unchanged (money neutrality).

↑r (Real interest rate rises) — the most-asked variable

↑r → cost of capital (r+δ) rises → fewer projects clear MPK > r+δ → ↓I (investment) → ↓PE → ↓Y → ↓Yd → ↓C → further ↓Y (multiplier)
In plain English. Higher rates make borrowing expensive → firms postpone investment → output falls → workers earn less → consumption falls → output falls more. Standard transmission of monetary tightening.

↑P (Price level rises)

↑P → ↓(M/P) (real money supply falls) → LM shifts LEFT → ↑r → ↓I → ↓Y
In plain English. Higher prices means the same nominal money supply buys less. Interest rates must rise to clear the now-tighter money market, choking investment. This is the mechanism behind the downward-sloping AD curve.

Open economy

↑e (Currency appreciates)

↑e → domestic goods MORE expensive abroad → ↓X (exports)
↑e → foreign goods CHEAPER at home → ↑IM (imports)
→ ↓NX (net exports) → ↓Y (floating ER)
In plain English. Stronger home currency → foreigners pay more for our goods, so they buy less → our exporters hurt. At the same time, we buy more foreign stuff because it's now cheaper. NX falls.

Tariff (Import tariff imposed)

Tariff → ↓IM → ↑NX at any e → IS* shifts RIGHT
Under floating: e appreciates, cancels NX gain → Y unchanged
Under fixed: CB expands M, LM* shifts right → Y rises
In plain English. Tariffs make foreign goods expensive → people buy domestic → net exports rise. Under fixed ER, the central bank expands M to prevent appreciation, so output rises. Under floating, the currency just appreciates and the gain evaporates.

Solow growth

↑s (Saving rate rises)

↑s → sf(k) curve shifts UP → ↑k* (steady-state capital per worker) → ↑y* (output per worker)
Short-run: ↓C; long-run: ↑c* (if below Golden Rule)
In plain English. Save more → build more machines per worker → each worker more productive → higher long-run output. But you sacrifice some consumption today. Trade-off: short-run pain for long-run gain.

↑n (Population growth rises)

↑n → break-even line (δ+n+g)k steepens → ↓k* → ↓y* (per worker)
Total Y grows faster (n+g); per-capita unchanged (g)
In plain English. More workers means capital is "diluted" across them. Each worker has less to work with, so output per worker falls. Total economy grows faster, but the average person is no better off.

↑g (Technology growth rises)

↑g → break-even steepens → ↓k*, ↓y* per effective worker
But long-run per-capita growth rate RISES (= g)
In plain English. Better technology means workers produce more, year after year. This is the ONLY thing that drives sustained per-capita growth in the long run — capital accumulation alone runs out of steam.

Aggregate supply & inflation

Adverse supply shock

↑input costs → SRAS shifts UP → ↑P AND ↓Y (STAGFLATION)
↑u (Okun) | ↑π despite ↓Y
In plain English. War, oil shock, pandemic → all firms face higher costs → prices rise but output falls. Both bad simultaneously. Policy dilemma: monetary easing fixes output but worsens inflation; tightening fights inflation but deepens recession.

↑u (Unemployment rises)

↑u → Okun: ↓Y (≈2% per 1pp ↑u) → ↓C → further ↓Y → Phillips: ↓π
In plain English. More unemployed people means less total production. Lost income reduces spending, hurting other businesses. The silver lining: inflation pressure eases (which is why central banks accept some u to fight high π).

↑πe (Expected inflation rises)

↑πe → Fisher: ↑i (nominal rates rise)
Wages bake in higher πe → actual π rises (self-fulfilling)
Phillips curve shifts UP
In plain English. When people EXPECT inflation, they act on it — workers demand bigger raises, landlords raise rents, firms raise prices preemptively. Inflation becomes self-fulfilling. This is why central banks fight to keep πe anchored.

PS1 — IS-LM (Closed Economy)

Explain what the IS curve represents and why it slopes downward.
The IS curve represents all combinations of income (Y) and the real interest rate (r) at which the goods market is in equilibrium — that is, where planned expenditure equals output. It slopes downward because a lower r raises investment I, which raises planned expenditure, which by the multiplier raises Y. So every lower r corresponds to a higher equilibrium Y.
Explain what the LM curve represents and why it slopes upward.
The LM curve represents all combinations of income (Y) and the real interest rate (r) at which the money market is in equilibrium — real money supply equals real money demand. It slopes upward because higher Y raises money demand (more transactions); with the real money supply M/P fixed, the interest rate r must rise to discourage additional money holding, restoring equilibrium.
What is the government expenditure multiplier?
The government expenditure multiplier measures how much equilibrium output Y rises per unit increase in government spending G. It equals 1/(1 − MPC), where MPC is the marginal propensity to consume. The multiplier exceeds 1 because each unit of new government spending generates further rounds of consumption through the spending cycle.
Explain crowding out.
Crowding out is the reduction in private investment that occurs when government spending rises. Higher G shifts IS right, raising both Y and r. The rise in r raises the cost of borrowing, so private firms invest less. The net effect on Y is therefore smaller than the simple multiplier would predict.

PS3 — Mundell-Fleming

Difference between fixed and floating exchange rates.
Under a fixed exchange rate regime, the central bank commits to maintaining e at a constant level by buying or selling foreign reserves. Money supply M becomes endogenous, so monetary policy independence is lost. Under a floating regime, e is determined by market forces, and the central bank can independently control M — but the exchange rate fluctuates with capital flows and trade balances.
Effect of fiscal expansion under floating ER.
The IS* curve shifts right, but LM* is vertical (Y is pinned by perfect capital mobility). Upward pressure on r attracts capital inflows; the currency appreciates. Higher e reduces NX, which exactly cancels the fiscal boost. Result: Y unchanged, e appreciates. Fiscal policy is INEFFECTIVE under floating ER.
Effect of monetary expansion under floating ER.
The LM* curve shifts right. Downward pressure on r causes capital outflows, so the currency depreciates. The depreciation makes exports cheap and imports expensive, raising NX. Higher NX raises Y. Result: Y rises, e depreciates. Monetary policy is EFFECTIVE under floating.
Effect of fiscal expansion under fixed ER.
The IS* curve shifts right. To prevent currency appreciation, the central bank sells domestic currency and buys foreign reserves, expanding M. The LM* shifts right alongside IS*. The new equilibrium has higher Y at the same fixed e. Result: Y rises (full multiplier), e remains fixed. Fiscal policy is EFFECTIVE under fixed ER.
Why is monetary policy ineffective under fixed ER?
An expansionary monetary policy would shift LM* right and depreciate the currency. To maintain the peg, the central bank must reverse by buying back its own currency, contracting M back to its original level. The LM* shift is undone. Result: Y unchanged.
Tariff under fixed ER — effect on NX, IS/LM, e, Y.
A tariff makes imports more expensive, raising NX at any given e. The IS* curve shifts right. Under fixed ER, the central bank must prevent the resulting appreciation by selling domestic currency, expanding M and shifting LM* right alongside. Result: Y rises, e fixed, NX rises. Caveat: this expansion of M may cause later inflationary pressure.
Why does appreciation reduce NX?
An appreciation makes domestic goods relatively more expensive in foreign markets, so foreigners buy fewer exports (X falls). At the same time, foreign goods become cheaper at home, so domestic consumers import more (IM rises). Net exports NX = X − IM fall on both sides. This is why IS* slopes downward in (e, Y) space.

PS4 — Solow Growth

Is steady-state k always equal to Golden Rule k? Why or why not?
No, not in general. The steady-state k* depends on the saving rate s (a choice). The Golden Rule kgold is determined by technology — it satisfies MPK = δ + n + g, the condition that maximises consumption per worker. They coincide only when s equals a specific Golden Rule rate. If s < sgold, the country is under-saving; if s > sgold, the country is over-saving.
How does a higher saving rate affect Solow steady state?
A higher saving rate raises steady-state capital per worker k* and output per worker y*. With more saving, more investment occurs at every k, shifting sf(k) up; the intersection with the break-even line moves right. The country reaches a higher level of output per worker. However, the long-run growth RATE of y per worker is unchanged — it always equals g (technology growth). Higher saving raises the level, not the rate.
How does higher population growth (n) affect Solow steady state?
Higher population growth n steepens the break-even line (δ+n+g)k — the economy needs more investment to equip the larger workforce. The intersection with sf(k) shifts leftward, lowering steady-state k* and y*. Total output Y grows faster (at n+g instead of g), but per-capita output is permanently lower.
Role of technology growth g in Solow with progress.
Technology growth g is the only source of sustained long-run growth in output per worker. While saving and population can change the level of per-capita output, they cannot sustain its growth rate — diminishing returns to capital eventually prevent that. Only continuous technological improvements raise productivity year after year, which is why advanced economies prioritise R&D, education, and innovation.

PS5 — Consumption Theory

Compare Keynes vs Friedman's PIH.
Keynes (1936) proposed that consumption depends on current disposable income: C = a + b·Yd, where b (MPC) is between 0 and 1. Friedman (1957) argued instead that consumption depends on permanent income YP: C = α·YP. Households smooth consumption over their lifetime expected income — transitory shocks are mostly saved, not spent. PIH explains the Kuznets puzzle (constant long-run APC, falling cross-section APC) better than Keynes.
Compare Keynes vs Modigliani's LCH.
Keynes (1936) said consumption depends on current disposable income. Modigliani (1954) argued that consumption depends on lifetime resources, not current income. People save during their working years and dissave in retirement to smooth consumption over their entire life: C = (W + R·Y)/T, where W is wealth, R is remaining working years, T is total years remaining. The implication is hump-shaped saving over the life cycle.
Explain the Kuznets puzzle.
Simon Kuznets observed that APC (C/Y) is roughly constant in long-run time-series data but falls as income rises in cross-section data. Keynes alone cannot explain this contradiction. PIH solves it: in cross-section, higher-income households have a larger transitory share, making their measured APC of permanent income look lower. In time-series, growth is mostly permanent, so APC stays constant.

Concept Definitions

Frictional vs structural unemployment.
Frictional unemployment arises from the time it takes for workers and jobs to find each other — job search, geographical moves. It is unavoidable but can be reduced through better job matching. Structural unemployment arises when wages are set above the market-clearing level (minimum wage, unions, efficiency wages), creating a persistent gap between labour supply and demand. Together they form the natural rate un.
Quantity Theory of Money — how does it determine inflation?
The Quantity Theory of Money states M × V = P × Y. In growth-rate form: %ΔM + %ΔV ≈ %ΔP + %ΔY. With V approximately constant in the long run, inflation equals money growth minus real output growth: %ΔP ≈ %ΔM − %ΔY. So if M grows at 8% and Y grows at 2%, inflation will be approximately 6%.
Money neutrality / classical dichotomy.
Money neutrality is the principle that in the long run, changes in the money supply affect only nominal variables (prices, nominal wages, nominal interest rates) — not real variables (output, employment, real wages). The classical dichotomy separates these two sets: real variables are determined by real factors (capital, labour, technology), while nominal variables are determined by monetary factors.
Stagflation — what is it and how does it arise?
Stagflation is the simultaneous occurrence of stagnant output (falling Y, rising unemployment) and high inflation (rising P). It typically arises from an adverse supply shock — oil price spike, war, pandemic — which raises input costs across the economy. The SRAS curve shifts up; the new equilibrium has higher P AND lower Y. Policy is stuck between fighting inflation and supporting output.
Time inconsistency in monetary policy.
Time inconsistency (Kydland-Prescott) is the central bank's incentive to deviate from announced policies once private agents commit. If the CB announces low inflation and workers set wages accordingly, the CB then has an incentive to engineer surprise inflation. Workers anticipate this and demand higher wages from the start, leading to high inflation without output gain. The solution is institutional commitment — making the CB independent and rule-based.

PS1 — IS-LM step-by-step recipe

Standard exam workflow:
  1. Solve Keynesian cross for Y with fixed I → identify the multiplier
  2. Replace I with I(r), derive IS curve
  3. Set money demand = supply, derive LM curve
  4. Solve IS = LM simultaneously for (Y*, r*)
  5. Apply policy shock, recompute new equilibrium
  6. Discuss "in your opinion" using economic logic

Worked example (S25 PS1 style)

Setup: C = 150 + 0.75(Y−T), I = 300, T = 150, G = 300. Later I = 500 − 150r. Money: (M/P)d = Y − 80r, M = 13,100, P = 10.

(a) Y in Keynesian cross with fixed I: Y = 150 + 0.75(Y−150) + 300 + 300
  0.25Y = 637.5 → Y* = 2,550

(b) IS with I = 500 − 150r: 0.25Y = 837.5 − 150r → IS: Y = 3,350 − 600r

(d) LM: 1,310 = Y − 80r → LM: Y = 1,310 + 80r

(f) Equilibrium: 3,350 − 600r = 1,310 + 80r → 2,040 = 680r → r* = 3%
  Y* = 1,310 + 80(3) = 1,550

PS4 — Solow steady-state recipe

Recipe (y = k1/2):
  1. Compute δ + n + g
  2. k* = (s / (δ+n+g))²
  3. y* = √k*
  4. Saving per worker = s · y*
  5. Consumption per worker c* = (1 − s) · y*
Golden Rule: MPK = δ + n + g (maximises c*).

Worked example

Country A: s = 0.15, n = 0.04, δ = 0.03, g = 0.02 → δ+n+g = 0.09
k* = (0.15/0.09)² ≈ 2.78
y* = √2.78 ≈ 1.667
sy* = 0.25 | c* = 0.85 × 1.667 = 1.417

General exam rules

  • Always show working. Partial credit is huge — make every step explicit.
  • Define terms before using them. "The IS curve represents…" before deriving.
  • For T/F: Answer first (TRUE or FALSE), then ONE sentence reasoning.
  • For "in your opinion": Anchor in economic theory; use frameworks (Y = C + I + G, IS-LM, Phillips, etc.).
  • Sketch diagrams. Label axes (Y horizontal; r/e/P vertical). Show curve shifts with arrows.
  • Sanity-check numbers. MPC > 1, negative Y, multiplier < 0 = computational error.
  • Don't panic on a confusing question. Skip, finish what you know, come back.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Confusing CPI weights (fixed) vs GDP deflator weights (current)
  • Confusing Okun's law sign (employment-Y positive, unemployment-Y negative)
  • Forgetting MPC ∈ (0, 1)
  • Drawing IS* as vertical (it's LM* that's vertical in Mundell-Fleming)
  • Forgetting all of (δ + n + g) in Solow steady-state denominator
  • Saying steady-state IS Golden Rule (only when s = sgold)
  • Confusing M (money supply) with M (imports) in open economy
  • Confusing T (taxes) with T (lifetime years) in LCH formula
