Universal recipes (memorise these — they recur every exam):
  • Keynesian cross: Y = C + I + G (+NX). Substitute → distribute → combine constants → isolate (1−MPC)·Y → divide.
  • IS derivation: Same as Keynesian cross but with I(r) = a − b·r → carry r to the end.
  • LM derivation: Compute M/P → set equal to demand → rearrange to Y = f(r).
  • IS-LM equilibrium: Set IS = LM → solve for r* → substitute into LM → cross-check on IS.
  • Solow steady state: sf(k*) = (δ+n+g)·k* → divide by k*1/2 → k*1/2 = s/(δ+n+g) → square → compute y*, sy*, c*.
  • Multipliers: G-multiplier = 1/(1−MPC); T-multiplier = −MPC/(1−MPC); balanced-budget = 1; open = 1/(1−MPC+m).
  • Fisher: i = r + π; r = i − π. QTM (growth): %ΔM + %ΔV = %ΔP + %ΔY.
  • Cobb-Douglas: MPL = (1−α)Y/L; MPK = αY/K; income shares = factor exponents.

PS1 — Jura-land IS-LM Case Study (30%)

VERBATIM EXAM QUESTION (S25 PS1): "Consider the closed economy of Jura-land described by the following equations: C = 150 + 0.75(Y − T), I = 300, T = 150, G = 300. (a) Find the equilibrium level of output Y. Now suppose investment depends on the interest rate: I = 500 − 150r. (b) Derive the IS curve. (c) Explain what the IS curve represents and the relationship between the two variables on the axes. The money market: (M/P)d = Y − 80r, M = 13,100, P = 10. (d) Derive the LM curve. (e) Explain what the LM curve represents. (f) Find the equilibrium level of output Y* and the interest rate r*. (g) Now suppose the government implements a contractionary fiscal policy by raising T from 150 to 300. Find the new equilibrium. (h) In your opinion, how will this policy affect society as a whole?"
Setup: Closed economy Jura-land.
C = 150 + 0.75(Y − T), I = 300 (initially fixed), T = 150, G = 300.
Later: I = 500 − 150r.
Money market: (M/P)d = Y − 80r, M = 13,100, P = 10 → M/P = 1,310.
Later: T raised to 300.

(a) Equilibrium Y in Keynesian cross with fixed I = 300 — step by step

Step 1. Equilibrium condition: Y = PE = C + I + G
Step 2. Substitute values (C = 150 + 0.75(Y−T), T = 150, I = 300, G = 300):
  Y = [150 + 0.75(Y − 150)] + 300 + 300
Step 3. Distribute 0.75 inside the bracket: 0.75(Y − 150) = 0.75Y − 112.5
  So Y = 150 + 0.75Y − 112.5 + 300 + 300
Step 4. Combine all constants on the right: 150 − 112.5 + 300 + 300 = 637.5
  So Y = 0.75Y + 637.5
Step 5. Subtract 0.75Y from both sides: Y − 0.75Y = 637.5 → 0.25Y = 637.5
Step 6. Divide both sides by 0.25 (= multiply by 4): Y* = 637.5 / 0.25 = 2,550
Why this works (concept). The Keynesian cross says output Y adjusts until total planned spending (PE) equals what's produced (Y). With prices fixed in the short run, any gap is closed by inventory changes that signal firms to expand or cut output. The autonomous part of the equation (a + I + G − MPC·T = 637.5 here) is multiplied by 1/(1−MPC) = 4 to give Y*. The factor 4 is the government expenditure multiplier — it amplifies any autonomous spending change through induced rounds of consumption. The 637.5 is what you'd consume + invest + spend if income were zero; the multiplier captures the full ripple.

(b) Derive IS with I = 500 − 150r — step by step

Step 1. Same equilibrium Y = C + I(r) + G, but I now depends on r.
Step 2. Substitute: Y = 150 + 0.75(Y − 150) + (500 − 150r) + 300
Step 3. Distribute 0.75: Y = 150 + 0.75Y − 112.5 + 500 − 150r + 300
Step 4. Combine constants: 150 − 112.5 + 500 + 300 = 837.5
  So Y = 0.75Y + 837.5 − 150r
Step 5. Subtract 0.75Y from both sides: 0.25Y = 837.5 − 150r
Step 6. Divide both sides by 0.25 (= multiply by 4): Y = 4(837.5) − 4(150r)
  IS: Y = 3,350 − 600r
Why the IS curve slopes down (concept). Each value of r determines an equilibrium Y. The slope coefficient −600 = −(multiplier × investment sensitivity) = −(4 × 150). A 1 pp drop in r raises I by 150, which raises Y by 150 × 4 = 600 through induced consumption. So lower r ↔ higher Y. The IS curve is the LOCUS of (Y, r) pairs where the goods market clears. We've moved from a single equilibrium Y* (in part a) to a whole curve, because I is no longer pinned down — it depends on r, which is determined by the money market.

(c) Explain IS curve (in words)

Model answer: The IS curve represents all combinations of income Y and the real interest rate r at which the goods market is in equilibrium — planned expenditure equals output. It slopes downward because lower r raises investment I, which raises planned expenditure, which by the multiplier raises Y. So every lower r corresponds to a higher equilibrium Y.

(d) Derive LM — step by step

Step 1. Money market equilibrium: real money supply = real money demand → M/P = (M/P)d.
Step 2. Compute real money supply: M/P = 13,100 / 10 = 1,310
Step 3. Set demand equal to supply: 1,310 = Y − 80r
Step 4. Solve for Y (add 80r to both sides): LM: Y = 1,310 + 80r
Why the LM curve slopes up (concept). Higher income Y means more transactions, so people want to hold more cash for spending → money demand rises. With real money supply (M/P) fixed by the central bank, the interest rate r must rise to make holding bonds more attractive than holding cash, restoring equilibrium. The slope coefficient (+80 in this LM equation) is the interest sensitivity of money demand: a 1 pp rise in r reduces money demand by 80, freeing up cash for transactions, which supports 80 units more Y. Key: the LM curve depends on M/P, not nominal M alone. When P changes (AD-AS), M/P changes → LM shifts.

(e) Explain LM curve (in words)

Model answer: The LM curve represents all (Y, r) combinations at which the money market is in equilibrium — real money supply equals real money demand. It slopes upward because higher Y raises money demand (more transactions); with real money supply fixed, r must rise to clear the market.

(f) Equilibrium IS = LM — step by step

Step 1. Both equations give Y, so set them equal: 3,350 − 600r = 1,310 + 80r
Step 2. Add 600r to both sides: 3,350 = 1,310 + 680r
Step 3. Subtract 1,310: 2,040 = 680r
Step 4. Divide by 680: r* = 2,040 / 680 = 3 (3%)
Step 5. Substitute r* into LM: Y* = 1,310 + 80(3) = 1,310 + 240 = 1,550
Step 6. Sanity check on IS: Y = 3,350 − 600(3) = 3,350 − 1,800 = 1,550 ✓
Why IS = LM gives THE equilibrium (concept). The IS curve alone is a whole locus of (Y, r) pairs where the goods market clears. The LM curve is a whole locus where the money market clears. Only one point (Y*, r*) lies on BOTH simultaneously — that's where the entire economy is in short-run equilibrium. The sanity check on IS isn't optional — it confirms you didn't make an algebra error: r* must produce the same Y* from either equation.

(g) Tax raised to T = 300 (contractionary fiscal) — step by step

Step 1. New IS with T = 300: Y = 150 + 0.75(Y − 300) + (500 − 150r) + 300
Step 2. Distribute 0.75: Y = 150 + 0.75Y − 225 + 500 − 150r + 300
Step 3. Combine constants: 150 − 225 + 500 + 300 = 725
  So Y = 0.75Y + 725 − 150r
Step 4. Subtract 0.75Y: 0.25Y = 725 − 150r
Step 5. Multiply by 4: IS': Y = 2,900 − 600r

Step 6. LM unchanged: Y = 1,310 + 80r
Step 7. Set IS' = LM: 2,900 − 600r = 1,310 + 80r
Step 8. Add 600r and subtract 1,310: 2,900 − 1,310 = 680r → 1,590 = 680r
Step 9. Divide by 680: r* = 1,590 / 680 ≈ 2.34 (2.34%)
Step 10. Substitute into LM: Y* = 1,310 + 80(2.34) ≈ 1,310 + 187 = 1,497

Result: Y falls from 1,550 → 1,497 (ΔY ≈ −53); r falls from 3% → 2.34% (Δr ≈ −0.66 pp). Tax hike → ↓C → IS shifts left → both Y and r fall.
Why both Y and r fall (concept). Higher T cuts disposable income → C falls → planned expenditure falls at every r → IS shifts LEFT (parallel shift of −600·ΔY = the new intercept). At the original r = 3%, output would fall sharply; but with lower Y, money demand drops, so r falls too, partly recovering I — that's the IS-LM dampening of the simple multiplier. Compared to the simple Keynesian cross: with no interest-rate channel, ΔY would equal the tax multiplier × ΔT = (−MPC/(1−MPC)) × 150 = −3 × 150 = −450. The actual IS-LM drop is only −53 — much smaller, because the falling r recovers some lost investment.

(h) Impact on society (model answer)

Model answer: Raising T reduces disposable income, lowering C and aggregate demand → Y falls from 1,550 to 1,497. Short-run impact: lower output likely means lower employment, hurting households' immediate welfare. However, lower r (from 3% to 2.34%) reduces crowding out, freeing some private investment, supporting long-run capital accumulation. Smaller deficit also slows debt accumulation, improving fiscal sustainability. Verdict: short-run pain for long-run gain. The wisdom of this policy depends on context — fine if the economy was overheating with inflation; harmful if already in recession.

PS3 — Mundell-Fleming: Denmark (fixed) vs Germany (floating) (20%)

VERBATIM EXAM QUESTION (S25 PS3): "Denmark has a fixed exchange rate, pegging the Danish krone (DKK) to the euro. Germany operates with a floating exchange rate (vs the US dollar). (a) Explain the difference between fixed and floating exchange rate regimes. (b) Germany implements an expansionary fiscal policy by raising government spending. Which curve(s) shift, and what is the effect on output and the exchange rate? (c) Denmark introduces import tariffs. Explain the effect on net exports, the IS or LM curve, the exchange rate, and output. (d) The Danish interest rate rises. Explain the impact on investment, consumption, and output."

(a) Difference between fixed and floating ER

Model answer: Under a fixed ER regime, the central bank commits to maintaining e constant against another currency by buying/selling reserves. Money supply M becomes endogenous, sacrificing monetary independence. Under floating ER, e is determined by market forces, and the CB can independently control M, but e fluctuates with capital flows and trade. Denmark pegs DKK to EUR (fixed). Germany within euro area floats against USD.

(b) Germany: expansionary fiscal under floating ER

Model answer: The IS* curve shifts right. But LM* is vertical (Y pinned by perfect capital mobility). The pressure on r attracts foreign capital → euro appreciates (e ↑). Appreciation reduces NX, which exactly cancels the fiscal boost. Result: Y unchanged, e appreciates. Fiscal policy is INEFFECTIVE under floating ER.

(c) Denmark: tariffs under fixed ER

Model answer: Tariffs raise NX at any given e → IS* shifts right. Under fixed ER, the central bank prevents the resulting appreciation by selling DKK and buying foreign reserves, expanding M. The LM* shifts right alongside IS*. Result: Y rises, e fixed, NX rises in equilibrium. Caveat: the expansion of M may build inflationary pressure, and trade partners may retaliate.

(d) Denmark: interest rate rises — impact on I, C, Y

Model answer: Direct effect: higher r raises cost of capital (r + δ), reducing investment I. Indirect effect: lower I lowers Y, which lowers Yd, which lowers C. Both I and C fall; G unchanged. Net: Y falls through both investment and consumption channels.

PS4 — Solow Growth, Country A vs B (20%)

VERBATIM EXAM QUESTION (S25 PS4): "Consider two countries with the same production function y = k1/2 and the same population growth rate n = 0.04. They differ in saving rate, depreciation, and technology growth. Country A: s = 0.15, δ = 0.03, g = 0.02. Country B: s = 0.20, δ = 0.03, g = 0.04. (a) For each country, compute the steady-state values of capital per worker k*, output per worker y*, saving per worker, and consumption per worker. (b) Discuss the differences between the two countries. (c) Is the steady-state level of capital always equal to the Golden Rule level? Why or why not?"
Setup: y = k1/2. Both countries: n = 0.04. Same n. Differ in s and g.
Country A: s = 0.15, δ = 0.03, g = 0.02 → δ + n + g = 0.09
Country B: s = 0.20, δ = 0.03, g = 0.04 → δ + n + g = 0.11

(a) Steady-state values — step by step for Country A

Step 1. Compute the break-even rate: δ + n + g = 0.03 + 0.04 + 0.02 = 0.09
Step 2. Steady state condition: sf(k*) = (δ+n+g)·k*. With y = k1/2:
  0.15·k*1/2 = 0.09·k*
Step 3. Divide both sides by k*1/2: 0.15 = 0.09·k*1/2
Step 4. Solve for k*1/2: k*1/2 = 0.15 / 0.09 = 1.667
Step 5. Square both sides: k*A = (1.667)² ≈ 2.78
Step 6. y*A = √k* = √2.78 ≈ 1.667
Step 7. Saving per worker = s·y* = 0.15 × 1.667 ≈ 0.250
Step 8. Consumption per worker c* = (1−s)·y* = 0.85 × 1.667 ≈ 1.417
Sanity check: sy* + c* = 0.250 + 1.417 = 1.667 = y* ✓
Why δ+n+g matters (concept). "Break-even investment" is what you need to invest just to keep k constant per worker. Three things eat it up: (1) δ = depreciation — old capital wears out; (2) n = population growth — new workers need to be equipped; (3) g = technology growth — efficiency units grow, so k per effective worker is diluted. Steady state is when saving exactly covers all three drains. The general closed-form solution: k* = (s / (δ+n+g))² when y = √k. Long-run growth rate of output PER WORKER = g (Solow's key result — only technology drives sustainable per-capita growth, not capital accumulation alone).

Steady-state values — step by step for Country B

Step 1. δ + n + g = 0.03 + 0.04 + 0.04 = 0.11
Step 2. sf(k*) = (δ+n+g)·k* → 0.20·k*1/2 = 0.11·k*
Step 3. Divide by k*1/2: 0.20 = 0.11·k*1/2
Step 4. k*1/2 = 0.20/0.11 ≈ 1.818
Step 5. Square: k*B ≈ 3.31
Step 6. y*B = √3.31 ≈ 1.818
Step 7. sy*B = 0.20 × 1.818 ≈ 0.364
Step 8. c*B = 0.80 × 1.818 ≈ 1.455

(b) Discussion

Model answer: Country B has a higher saving rate (0.20 vs 0.15) AND a higher technology growth (0.04 vs 0.02). Higher s raises sf(k), shifting the saving curve up → higher k* and y*. Higher g raises (δ+n+g), making break-even investment steeper — partially offsetting the s effect. Net: B has higher k*, y* and c*. Long-run per-capita growth rate equals g, so B grows faster (4% vs 2%). Levels AND growth favour B in this case.

(c) Golden Rule comparison

Model answer: No, steady-state k* is not always at the Golden Rule. The steady-state depends on the saving rate s (a choice). The Golden Rule kgold is determined by technology — it satisfies MPK = δ + n + g and maximises consumption per worker. They coincide only when s equals a specific Golden Rule saving rate. If s < sgold: under-saving (raising s raises c* eventually). If s > sgold: over-saving (lowering s raises c* immediately).

PS5 — Consumption + Danish CPI (20%)

VERBATIM EXAM QUESTION (S25 PS5): "(a) Explain Friedman's Permanent Income Hypothesis (PIH) and discuss how it differs from Keynes's consumption function. The table below shows annual inflation rates by CPI category in Denmark. (b) Which category shows the largest fluctuation in inflation? Compute the difference between maximum and minimum. (c) Why does the inflation rate in the 'Education' category remain unchanged? (d) Why is 'Restaurants and hotels' particularly volatile? (e) If housing and energy inflation outpaces wage growth, how would this affect aggregate output Y? Use the Y = C + I + G framework."

(a) Friedman's PIH vs Keynes

Model answer (3 paragraphs):

Keynes (1936) Absolute Income Hypothesis: C = a + b·Yd, where b (MPC) ∈ (0,1). Current disposable income is the main determinant. APC = a/Y + b falls as Y rises.

Friedman (1957) PIH: C = α · YP. Consumption depends on PERMANENT (long-run expected) income. Transitory shocks mostly saved; lottery windfalls don't raise C much. Implication: temporary tax rebates have minimal effect on C, while Keynes predicts a multiplier × rebate effect.

Differences and the Kuznets puzzle: Empirically, APC is constant in long-run time-series but falls in cross-section. Keynes alone can't explain. PIH solves it: cross-section higher incomes have larger transitory share → measured APC looks lower. Time-series growth is mostly permanent → constant APC.

(b) Most fluctuating CPI category

"Restaurants and hotels" (#11) showed the largest fluctuation in inflation. Compute max − min: 2.6 − 0.6 = 2.0 pp range. Indicates a market sensitive to: labour costs (labour-intensive), seasonal demand patterns, energy and food input cost pass-through, and elastic discretionary demand.

(c) Why "Education" stayed unchanged

Education prices are largely public-sector administered. Tuition fees are regulated and adjusted on programmed annual cycles, not in response to monthly market shocks. Teacher salaries are set in multi-year collective bargaining agreements. Hence flat 3.7-3.9% reflecting the policy-set annual adjustment.

(d) Why "Restaurants and hotels" volatile

Labour-intensive (wage shocks transmit fast), seasonal demand (Christmas, summer holidays), energy/food cost pass-through directly into menu prices, elastic discretionary demand. These factors combine to make this category one of the most volatile in CPI.

(e) Effect on Y of housing/energy inflation when wages stagnant

Higher inflation in essentials reduces real disposable income. Households cannot reduce essentials (inelastic), so they cut discretionary spending. Real C falls. From Y = C + I + G: lower C reduces aggregate demand, reducing Y. The central bank may tighten (↑r → ↓I), further reducing Y. The burden falls disproportionately on low-income households (essentials are a larger share of their budget).

PS1 — Eco-land Open Economy (30%)

VERBATIM EXAM QUESTION (S25_RE PS1): "Consider the open economy of Eco-land described by Y = C + I + G + NX. The economy is currently characterised by: C = 400 + 0.70(Y − T), I = 1,000, T = 400, G = 800, X = 800, M = 800 (so NX = 0). (a) Find the equilibrium level of output Y and consumption C. (b) What is the government expenditure multiplier? The government wishes to reduce its budget deficit. Two options are considered: Option 1: Reduce G by 400. Option 2: Raise T to 800. (c) For each option, calculate the new equilibrium level of Y and C. (d) Which policy would you prefer? Justify your answer. (e) Explain why net exports NX have a negative relationship with the exchange rate e."
Setup: Open economy Eco-land. Y = C + I + G + NX.
C = 400 + 0.70(Y − T), I = 1000, T = 400, G = 800.
X = M = 800 → NX = 0.
Then evaluate policies: (i) reduce G by 400; (ii) raise T to 800.

(a) Equilibrium Y and C — step by step

Step 1. Open-economy equilibrium: Y = C + I + G + NX
Step 2. Substitute values (C = 400 + 0.70(Y−T), T = 400, I = 1,000, G = 800, NX = 0):
  Y = [400 + 0.70(Y − 400)] + 1,000 + 800 + 0
Step 3. Distribute 0.70: 0.70(Y − 400) = 0.70Y − 280
  So Y = 400 + 0.70Y − 280 + 1,000 + 800
Step 4. Combine constants: 400 − 280 + 1,000 + 800 = 1,920
  So Y = 0.70Y + 1,920
Step 5. Subtract 0.70Y from both sides: 0.30Y = 1,920
Step 6. Divide by 0.30: Y* = 1,920 / 0.30 = 6,400
Step 7. Compute C: Yd = Y − T = 6,400 − 400 = 6,000; C = 400 + 0.70(6,000) = 400 + 4,200 = 4,600

(b) Multiplier

The government expenditure multiplier measures how Y responds to a unit change in G. It equals 1/(1 − MPC). Here MPC = 0.70, so multiplier = 1/(1 − 0.70) = 1/0.30 ≈ 3.33. A unit rise in G raises Y by 3.33 units through successive rounds of induced consumption.

(c) Policy options — calculate new Y and C

Option 1: Reduce G by 400 (so new G = 400) — step by step
Step 1. Equilibrium: Y = C + I + G + NX with G = 400 now
Step 2. Substitute: Y = 400 + 0.70(Y − 400) + 1,000 + 400 + 0
Step 3. Distribute 0.70: Y = 400 + 0.70Y − 280 + 1,000 + 400
Step 4. Combine constants: 400 − 280 + 1,000 + 400 = 1,520
  So Y = 0.70Y + 1,520
Step 5. Subtract 0.70Y: 0.30Y = 1,520
Step 6. Divide by 0.30: Y = 1,520 / 0.30 ≈ 5,066.67 (drop of 1,333.33)
Step 7. Compute C: C = 400 + 0.70(5,066.67 − 400) = 400 + 0.70(4,666.67) = 400 + 3,266.67 = 3,666.67
Verification: ΔY = ΔG × multiplier = −400 × 3.33 = −1,333.33 ✓
Option 2: Raise T to 800 (so ΔT = +400) — step by step
Step 1. Equilibrium: Y = C + I + G + NX with T = 800 now
Step 2. Substitute: Y = 400 + 0.70(Y − 800) + 1,000 + 800 + 0
Step 3. Distribute 0.70: 0.70(Y − 800) = 0.70Y − 560
  So Y = 400 + 0.70Y − 560 + 1,000 + 800
Step 4. Combine constants: 400 − 560 + 1,000 + 800 = 1,640
  So Y = 0.70Y + 1,640
Step 5. Subtract 0.70Y: 0.30Y = 1,640
Step 6. Divide by 0.30: Y = 1,640 / 0.30 ≈ 5,466.67 (drop of 933.33)
Step 7. Compute C: C = 400 + 0.70(5,466.67 − 800) = 400 + 0.70(4,666.67) = 400 + 3,266.67 = 3,666.67
Verification: ΔY = ΔT × (−MPC/(1−MPC)) = 400 × (−2.33) = −933.33 ✓
Why these two options matter together (concept). Both options "save" the same 400 units of government deficit: Option 1 cuts G by 400 (deficit shrinks by 400); Option 2 raises T by 400 (deficit shrinks by 400). But the GDP cost differs sharply because the multipliers differ:
  • G-multiplier in absolute value: 1/(1−MPC) = 3.33
  • T-multiplier in absolute value: MPC/(1−MPC) = 2.33
The G-multiplier is exactly 1 unit larger than the T-multiplier. Why? Because ΔG enters demand directly (full 400), while ΔT first goes through consumption (only MPC·400 = 280 of it lowers C, and only THAT amount is multiplied). The tax change "skips" one round of spending compared to a direct G change. Exam takeaway: when comparing fiscal options to close a deficit, raising T always hurts Y by LESS than cutting G of equal magnitude. The flip side: when stimulating, ↑G is more powerful per euro than ↓T. Same consumption result here: C drops to 3,666.67 in BOTH options — coincidence of these numbers; not a general rule.

(d) Which policy is preferred?

Model answer: Option 2 (raising T) reduces Y by less (933 vs 1,333) than Option 1 (reducing G). Both result in the same C (3,666.67). The reason: government spending multiplier (1/(1−MPC) = 3.33) is LARGER in absolute value than the tax multiplier (MPC/(1−MPC) = 2.33), because a tax change first goes through the consumption function (only MPC × ΔT translates to ↓C) before the multiplier kicks in. Prefer Option 2 if minimising GDP loss is the goal. However, raising taxes may have political costs and distortionary effects on labour supply.

(e) Negative NX-e relationship

Model answer: When the exchange rate appreciates (↑e — home currency stronger), domestic goods become more expensive abroad, so foreigners buy fewer exports (↓X). At the same time, foreign goods become cheaper at home, so domestic consumers buy more imports (↑IM). Net exports NX = X − IM fall on both sides. This negative relationship is why the IS* curve in (Y, e) space slopes downward.

VERBATIM EXAM QUESTION (S25_RE PS3): "Consider a closed economy described by the IS-LM model. (a) Explain why the LM curve slopes upward and the IS curve slopes downward. (b) What is the Quantity Theory of Money, and how does it explain the long-run determination of the price level? (c) Use the IS-LM framework to explain the effect of an expansionary monetary policy (↑M) on output and the interest rate. (d) Use the IS-LM framework to explain the effect of an expansionary fiscal policy (↑G or ↓T). Discuss crowding out."

(a) Why LM slopes up and IS slopes down

Model answer:

IS slopes down: The IS curve shows (Y, r) combinations where the goods market clears. When r falls, investment I rises (lower cost of borrowing), raising planned expenditure and Y via the multiplier. So lower r → higher Y → downward slope.

LM slopes up: The LM curve shows (Y, r) combinations where the money market clears. When Y rises, money demand rises (more transactions). With M/P fixed, r must rise to discourage additional money holding. So higher Y → higher r → upward slope.

(b) QTM and the price level

Model answer: QTM states M × V = P × Y. With V and real Y constant in the long run, %ΔP = %ΔM. If the central bank increases M, in the SHORT run with sticky prices, real M/P rises temporarily — but in the LONG run, P rises proportionally. Real money supply M/P returns to its original level. This is money neutrality: M affects only nominal variables long-run.

(c) Expansionary monetary policy in IS-LM

Model answer: Expansionary monetary policy means ↑M. In the short run with P sticky, real money supply M/P rises. The LM curve shifts RIGHT (or DOWN). With the IS curve unchanged, the new equilibrium has lower r and higher Y. The lower r encourages investment, which raises Y through the multiplier. Result: Y rises, r falls.

(d) Expansionary fiscal policy in IS-LM

Model answer: Expansionary fiscal policy means ↑G or ↓T. The IS curve shifts RIGHT (at any r, planned expenditure is higher → higher Y). With LM unchanged, the new equilibrium has higher r and higher Y. The rise in r reduces some private investment — this is crowding out. Net effect: Y rises (but by less than the simple multiplier would predict), r rises.

VERBATIM EXAM QUESTION (V22 PS2): "Consider the Cobb-Douglas production function Y = A·Lα·Kβ with α = β = 1/2, A = 1, K = L = 1,000. (i) What returns to scale does this production function display? Y = A·Lα·Kβ. α = β = 1/2. A = 1, K = L = 1,000.

(i) RTS?

α + β = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. Constant Returns to Scale (CRS). Doubling all inputs doubles output.
Why α + β tells you the RTS (concept). For Y = A·Lα·Kβ, scale all inputs by t: Y(tL, tK) = A·(tL)α·(tK)β = t(α+β)·A·Lα·Kβ = t(α+β)·Y. So if α+β = 1 (CRS), doubling inputs (t=2) gives 21·Y = 2Y exactly. If α+β > 1 (IRS), gives 21.x·Y > 2Y. If α+β < 1 (DRS), gives less. This sum test is the quickest way to identify RTS from a Cobb-Douglas function. Exam trap: Note here labour exponent is written FIRST (Lα·Kβ) — different problems flip the convention, so always check which letter goes with which factor.

(ii) MPL and MPK — step by step

Step 1. Take partial of Y w.r.t. L: MPL = ∂Y/∂L = α·A·Lα−1·Kβ
Step 2. Substitute α = β = 1/2, A = 1: MPL = (1/2)·L−1/2·K1/2
Step 3. Combine: MPL = (1/2)·(K/L)1/2

Step 4. Take partial of Y w.r.t. K: MPK = ∂Y/∂K = β·A·Lα·Kβ−1
Step 5. Substitute: MPK = (1/2)·L1/2·K−1/2
Step 6. Combine: MPK = (1/2)·(L/K)1/2

(iii) Calculate Y with K = L = 1,000 — step by step

Step 1. Y = A·Lα·Kβ = 1 × 1,0001/2 × 1,0001/2
Step 2. Compute √1,000 ≈ 31.62
Step 3. Multiply: Y = 31.62 × 31.62 = 1,000
Alternative shortcut: 1,0001/2 × 1,0001/2 = 1,0001/2 + 1/2 = 1,0001 = 1,000

(iv) Labour and capital income — step by step

Step 1. Plug into MPL formula: MPL = (1/2)·(K/L)1/2 = (1/2)·(1,000/1,000)1/2 = (1/2)·1 = 0.5
Step 2. Labour income = MPL × L = 0.5 × 1,000 = 500
Step 3. Plug into MPK formula: MPK = (1/2)·(L/K)1/2 = (1/2)·1 = 0.5
Step 4. Capital income = MPK × K = 0.5 × 1,000 = 500
Step 5. Total: 500 + 500 = 1,000 = Y ✓ (Euler's theorem for CRS)

PS3 — Short-answer concepts (20%)

VERBATIM EXAM QUESTION (V22 PS3): "(i) Explain the difference between nominal GDP and real GDP. Which is better for measuring economic performance? (ii) Explain how an increase in money growth raises inflation according to the Quantity Theory of Money. (iii) Compare Keynes's consumption function with Modigliani's Life-Cycle Hypothesis. How will this shock affect the natural rate of output Y̅, the natural rate of unemployment un, and the price level P in the short run? (i) Nominal vs Real GDP

Nominal GDP values output at current prices; Real GDP values it at constant base-year prices. Real GDP is better for measuring economic performance because it strips out inflation — changes reflect actual production, not price changes.

(ii) Money growth and inflation (QTM)

QTM: M·V = P·Y. In growth rates: %ΔM + %ΔV = %ΔP + %ΔY. With V and real Y constant in the long run, %ΔP = %ΔM (one-to-one). So an extra 1% money growth produces 1% extra inflation.

(iii) Keynes vs Modigliani's LCH

Keynes: C = a + b·Yd (short-run focus on current income). LCH: C = (W + R·Y)/T (lifetime smoothing). Short-run: Keynes predicts APC falls with income; LCH predicts smoother consumption. Long-run: LCH explains the Kuznets puzzle (constant APC) while Keynes alone cannot.

(iv) Russia-Ukraine supply shock (AD-AS)

An adverse supply shock shifts SRAS up/left. Short-run: ↓Y AND ↑P (stagflation). ↑u (Okun). Natural rate Y̅ and un unchanged in the short run (LRAS doesn't shift). Long-run: wages and Pe adjust, SRAS drifts back, but P stays permanently higher.

Policy options: (1) Expansionary monetary (↑M) shifts AD right → Y recovers but inflation worsens. (2) Supply-side intervention (energy subsidies) shifts SRAS back without adding to demand pressure. (3) Wait — slow but natural adjustment.

Step 2. Substitute knowns (%ΔM = 6%, %ΔY = 4%): 6% = π + 4%
Step 3. Solve for π: π = 6% − 4% = 2%
Step 4. Apply Fisher equation: i = r + π
Step 5. Substitute (r = 1%, π = 2%): i = 1% + 2% = 3%
Why this two-step approach works (concept). To find i, you need π. You're given r (real rate, from preferences/productivity) and need to derive π from monetary data. QTM links the two: with V constant, money growth in EXCESS of real-output growth becomes inflation. Once you have π, just add it to r. This combination (QTM → π → Fisher → i) is one of the most reliable exam patterns — appears in V19_RE, V22_RE, V20_RE.

Common Solution Patterns (older exams V19, V20, V21)

The older exams (V19, V20, V21) had different structures, but the underlying problems recur. Here are the key patterns and answers.

Pattern 1: Keynesian cross with multiplier

Given C = a + b(Y-T), I, T, G. Find equilibrium Y, then multiplier and policy shocks.
Recipe:
1. Solve Y = C + I + G with substitution
2. Multiplier = 1/(1−MPC); apply ΔG × multiplier or ΔT × (−MPC/(1−MPC)) for policy shocks
3. Check Spriv = Y − T − C; Spub = T − G; Stotal should equal I (closed) or I + NX (open)

Pattern 2: Cobb-Douglas income shares

Y = A·Kα·Lβ. Given values, find MPL, MPK, factor incomes.
Recipe:
MPL = αY/L (NOT (1−α)Y/L if Y = KαL1−α!! Check the exponent)
Labour income = MPL × L = (exponent on L) × Y
Capital income = MPK × K = (exponent on K) × Y
Sum = Y exactly if α + β = 1 (CRS)

Pattern 3: Mundell-Fleming policy choice

Compare options: (a) ↑G, (b) ↓r via monetary, (c) ↑NX via trade. Which works under given regime?
Fixed ER:
• (a) ↑G: works (fiscal multiplier applies; CB expands M)
• (b) ↓r monetary: impossible (CB must defend peg)
• (c) ↑NX trade: works (CB expands M to defend peg)
Floating ER:
• (a) ↑G: no effect (currency appreciates, NX cancels)
• (b) ↓r monetary: works (currency depreciates, NX rises)
• (c) ↑NX trade: no effect (currency appreciates, cancels)

Pattern 4: Solow with two countries (or two periods)

y = k1/2. Compute k*, y*, sy*, c* under different parameter sets.
Recipe:
1. Compute δ + n + g for each country/period
2. k* = (s/(δ+n+g))²
3. y* = √k*
4. sy* = s·y*
5. c* = (1−s)·y*
Discussion (always): Golden Rule condition MPK = δ+n+g; steady state ≠ Golden Rule unless s = sgold.

Pattern 5: Consumption theory comparison

Compare Keynes with Friedman PIH or Modigliani LCH.
3-paragraph structure:
1. State Keynes: C = a + b·Yd; current income; APC falls with Y.
2. State the other theory (PIH or LCH): forward-looking, lifetime/permanent income smoothing.
3. Discuss differences (Kuznets puzzle, policy implications for tax rebates).

Pattern 6: AD-AS supply shock

Adverse supply shock (oil, war, pandemic). Effect on Ȳ, un, P short run. Policy response.
Standard answer:
• Ȳ unchanged (LRAS doesn't shift short-run).
• un unchanged (depends on labour market structure).
• Short-run: SRAS up → ↓Y, ↑P, ↑u (stagflation).
• Long-run: SRAS drifts back; Y returns to Ȳ; P stays elevated.
• Policy: ↑M restores Y but worsens π; or supply-side intervention; or wait.

Pattern 7: Fisher equation calculation

i = r + π. Given i and CPI change, find r.
Recipe:
1. π = (CPInew − CPIold) / CPIold × 100
2. r = i − π

Pattern 8: QTM with growth rates

M·V = P·Y. Given M growth, Y growth, find π.
Recipe (V constant):
%ΔP = %ΔM − %ΔY
e.g., %ΔM = 8%, %ΔY = 2% → π ≈ 6%

Common pitfalls / trap warnings (read before the exam!)

  1. Tax multiplier sign. The TAX multiplier is −MPC/(1−MPC) (negative — a tax HIKE reduces Y). The G multiplier is +1/(1−MPC) (positive). Don't confuse them. The G multiplier is ALWAYS 1 unit larger in absolute value than the T multiplier — that's the basis of the balanced-budget multiplier = 1.
  2. Cobb-Douglas exponent ordering. Y = KαL1−α with α = capital share is the standard convention. But V22 PS2 writes Y = LαKβ — labour exponent first. ALWAYS check which exponent goes with which factor before applying MPL = (exponent on L) × Y/L. Get this wrong and you swap capital/labour shares.
  3. CRS/IRS/DRS direction. If "doubling inputs raises Y from 120 to 180," the ratio is 180/120 = 1.5, less than 2 → DRS, NOT IRS. The exam phrasing can sound like IRS at first read. Compute the ratio explicitly.
  4. Real vs nominal interest rate. Fisher: i = r + π. Don't substitute i where you need r (e.g., in I = a − b·r). Some problems give you i and inflation and ask for r (= i − π).
  5. CPI vs GDP deflator. CPI uses FIXED basket (Laspeyres). Deflator uses CURRENT quantities (Paasche). Don't reverse them — common T/F trap.
  6. Money multiplier m vs marginal propensity to import m. Same letter, totally different concepts. Money multiplier = 1/rr (reserves ratio). Marginal propensity to import is in the open-economy multiplier 1/(1−MPC+m). Context tells you which.
  7. LCH formula T. C = (W + R·Y)/T — here T = total remaining years of life, NOT taxes. Don't substitute the value of taxes here.
  8. "Employment and Y" vs "unemployment and Y". Okun's law says ↑u ↔ ↓Y (negative). But ↑employment ↔ ↑Y (positive). T/F traps abound — V23_RE PS2d caught students on this.
  9. Mundell-Fleming policy effectiveness. Memorise the matrix: under FLOATING only monetary works; under FIXED only fiscal and trade work. Getting the regime wrong inverts the answer.
  10. Golden Rule vs steady state. They are NOT the same unless s = sgold. Steady state depends on the chosen saving rate; Golden Rule is the s that maximises c*. Standard answer to "is steady state = Golden Rule": NO, only at specific s.
  11. NX and exchange rate. ↑e (appreciation) → ↓X, ↑M → ↓NX. Don't reverse the sign.
  12. Sanity check IS-LM. After solving for (Y*, r*), always plug r* back into BOTH equations. If you get the same Y, your algebra is right. If they differ, find your mistake.
