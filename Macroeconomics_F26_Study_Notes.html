1. Formula Cheat Sheet (Bring This Page)

Print this page separately and keep it on the desk. Every formula in the exam is here.

National Income Identity & Multipliers

Y = C + I + G  (closed)   |   Y = C + I + G + NX  (open)
C = a + b·(Y − T)   where b = MPC ∈ (0,1)
Disposable income: Yd = Y − T
Private saving: Spriv = Y − T − C  |  Public saving: Spub = T − G
National saving: S = Spriv + Spub = Y − C − G
Government-spending multiplier: ΔY/ΔG = 1/(1 − MPC)
Tax multiplier: ΔY/ΔT = −MPC/(1 − MPC)
Balanced-budget multiplier: ΔG = ΔT → ΔY = ΔG (it equals 1)

IS-LM Model (closed economy)

IS curve: derived from Y = C(Y − T) + I(r) + G  → negative slope in (Y, r)
LM curve: derived from M/P = L(Y, r), often L = Y − cr  → positive slope in (Y, r)
Equilibrium: solve IS = LM for Y* and r*
Fiscal expansion (↑G or ↓T): IS shifts right → Y↑, r↑ (crowding out: I falls)
Monetary expansion (↑M): LM shifts right → Y↑, r↓

Mundell-Fleming (open economy, perfect capital mobility, r = r*)

NX = NX(e),   ↑e (appreciation) → ↓NX
Floating ER: Fiscal policy ineffective (e adjusts); Monetary policy effective.
Fixed ER: Fiscal policy effective; Monetary policy ineffective (M endogenous).
LM* is vertical at r = r*; IS* slopes downward in (e, Y).

Classical Production & Factor Markets

Cobb-Douglas: Y = A·Lα·Kβ. RTS: if α + β = 1 → CRS; >1 → IRS; <1 → DRS.
MPL = ∂Y/∂L = α·Y/L  |  MPK = ∂Y/∂K = β·Y/K
Real wage W/P = MPL  |  Real rent R/P = MPK
Labor income share = MPL · L = αY  |  Capital income share = MPK · K = βY

Money Market & Fisher

Quantity theory: M·V = P·Y  → %ΔM + %ΔV = %ΔP + %ΔY  → %ΔP ≈ %ΔM − %ΔY (V constant)
Fisher equation: i = r + πe (nominal = real + expected inflation)
Real money balances: M/P; Real money demand: (M/P)d = L(Y, i)

Solow Growth Model

Production per worker: y = f(k) = k1/2 (square-root form is the standard exam case)
Δk = s·f(k) − (δ + n + g)·k   (capital accumulation per effective worker)
Steady state: Δk = 0 → s·k*1/2 = (δ + n + g)·k* → k* = (s/(δ + n + g))²
y* = √k*  |  saving per worker = s·y*  |  consumption per worker c* = (1−s)·y*
Golden rule: k where consumption is maximised → MPK = δ + n + g

Inflation, CPI, Phillips, Okun

CPI uses fixed basket weights (Laspeyres). GDP deflator uses current quantities (Paasche).
GDP deflator = Nominal GDP / Real GDP × 100
Inflation: π = (Pt − Pt−1) / Pt−1 × 100
Short-run Phillips curve: π = πe − β(u − un) + ν
Okun's law: ΔY/Y ≈ −β·(u − un); employment ↑ → output ↑ (positive); unemployment ↑ → output ↓ (negative)

Consumption Theories

Keynes: C = a + b·Yd. APC = a/Y + b falls as Y rises. Short-run focused on current disposable income.
Friedman PIH: C = α·YP (permanent income). Transitory shocks barely affect C.
Modigliani LCH: C = (W + R·Y)/T (smooth over lifetime). Wealth + future income drive consumption.

Problem Set 1 (≈30%) — Keynesian Cross & IS-LM CASE STUDY

What to expect. A multi-part case study set in a fictional country. Almost always: (a) Keynesian cross with fixed I → solve for Y, C, Spriv, Spub, multiplier; (b) replace fixed I with I = a − br, derive IS; (c) money market given (M/P)d = Y − cr, derive LM; (d) solve IS = LM for Y* and r*; (e) policy shock (↑G, ↓T, ↑/↓M) — recompute and explain effect.

1.1 The Goods Market — Keynesian Cross

The Keynesian cross plots planned expenditure (PE) against income (Y). Equilibrium occurs where PE = Y (the 45° line meets the PE line).

PE = C + I + G
C = a + b·(Y − T)   [a = autonomous consumption, b = MPC]
At equilibrium: Y = a + b·(Y − T) + I + G
Solving: Y* = (a − b·T + I + G) / (1 − b)

1.2 Multipliers (memorize these)

MultiplierFormulaIntuition
Government spending1 / (1 − MPC)If MPC = 0.75, multiplier = 4. ΔG = 100 → ΔY = 400.
Tax−MPC / (1 − MPC)Negative because ↑T lowers Y. Smaller (in absolute value) than gov spending multiplier.
Balanced budget (ΔG = ΔT)1The two effects partially cancel; net ΔY = ΔG exactly.

1.3 Deriving the IS Curve

If investment depends on the interest rate (I = a − br, with b > 0), then for each r we can solve the goods-market equilibrium for Y. The resulting Y(r) relationship is the IS curve.

Y = C + I(r) + G = a + b(Y − T) + (I0 − br) + G
Solve for Y: Y = [a − b·T + I0 − b·r + G] / (1 − b)

What to say in the explanation question (always asked): "The IS curve represents combinations of income (Y) and the real interest rate (r) at which the goods market is in equilibrium (planned expenditure = actual output). It is downward-sloping because a lower r raises investment, which raises planned expenditure, which by the multiplier raises Y."

1.4 Deriving the LM Curve

The money market is in equilibrium when real money demand equals real money supply: M/P = L(Y, r).

Real money demand: (M/P)d = Y − c·r  (typical exam form)
Real money supply: (M/P)s = M/P (M and P given as numbers)
Set demand = supply: M/P = Y − c·r → solve for r in terms of Y, or Y in terms of r:
r = (Y − M/P) / c  or  Y = M/P + c·r

Explanation answer: "The LM curve represents combinations of income (Y) and the real interest rate (r) at which the money market is in equilibrium. It is upward-sloping: when Y rises, money demand rises; with money supply fixed, the interest rate must rise to restore equilibrium."

1.5 Solving the IS-LM System

Equilibrium: set IS = LM and solve simultaneously for Y* and r*. Steps:

  1. Write IS as Y = f(r) and LM as Y = g(r).
  2. Set f(r) = g(r) and solve for r*.
  3. Substitute r* back into either equation to find Y*.
  4. Sanity-check: plug both into IS and LM separately, both should hold.

1.6 Policy Shocks (the part worth most marks)

PolicyCurveEffect on Y, rWhat to say in writing
↑G (expansionary fiscal)IS shifts rightY↑, r↑Higher G raises planned expenditure, raising Y. Higher Y raises money demand, raising r. The rise in r reduces I (crowding out), so ΔY < multiplier × ΔG.
↑T (contractionary fiscal)IS shifts leftY↓, r↓Higher taxes lower disposable income → lower C → lower planned expenditure → lower Y. With less Y, money demand falls → r falls.
↑M (expansionary monetary)LM shifts rightY↑, r↓More real money supply → at any Y, r must fall to restore money-market equilibrium. Lower r raises I → higher Y.
↓M (contractionary monetary)LM shifts leftY↓, r↑Symmetric mirror of ↑M.
Crowding out is the term for the partial offset of fiscal expansion by higher r → lower I. Always mention it when explaining a fiscal shock under IS-LM.

1.7 Worked Solutions for the Three Most-Likely PS1 Questions

★ Worked Example 1 — Full IS-LM (mirroring S25 PS1)

Setup: Closed economy. C = 150 + 0.75(Y − T), I = 300, T = 150, G = 300. Money demand: (M/P)d = Y − 80r. M = 13,100, P = 10. Later I = 500 − 150r.

(a) Equilibrium Y in the Keynesian cross (with fixed I = 300):

Y = C + I + G = 150 + 0.75(Y − 150) + 300 + 300
Y = 150 + 0.75Y − 112.5 + 300 + 300
Y − 0.75Y = 637.5
0.25Y = 637.5 → Y* = 2,550

(b) Derive IS using I = 500 − 150r:

Y = 150 + 0.75(Y − 150) + (500 − 150r) + 300
Y = 150 + 0.75Y − 112.5 + 500 − 150r + 300
0.25Y = 837.5 − 150r
IS: Y = 3,350 − 600r

(c) Explain IS curve: The IS curve shows all (Y, r) combinations where the goods market is in equilibrium. It slopes down because lower r raises I, which raises planned expenditure, which raises Y (via the multiplier).

(d) Derive LM using (M/P)d = Y − 80r and M/P = 13,100/10 = 1,310:

1,310 = Y − 80r → LM: Y = 1,310 + 80r

(e) Explain LM curve: The LM curve shows all (Y, r) where the money market is in equilibrium. It slopes up because higher Y raises money demand; with M/P fixed, r must rise to clear the market.

(f) Equilibrium IS = LM:

3,350 − 600r = 1,310 + 80r
3,350 − 1,310 = 80r + 600r
2,040 = 680r → r* = 3 (i.e. 3%)
Y* = 1,310 + 80·3 = 1,550

(g) Contractionary fiscal: T raised from 150 to 300. New equilibrium:

New IS: Y = 150 + 0.75(Y − 300) + (500 − 150r) + 300
0.25Y = 725 − 150r → IS: Y = 2,900 − 600r
LM unchanged: Y = 1,310 + 80r
2,900 − 600r = 1,310 + 80r → 1,590 = 680r → r* ≈ 2.34
Y* = 1,310 + 80·2.34 ≈ 1,497

(h) Effect on society: Raising T reduces disposable income, lowers C, lowers AD → output falls (Y from 1,550 to 1,497). Lower output usually means lower employment short-run, so the contractionary fiscal hurts households' immediate welfare. However, lower r could free up some private investment via reduced crowding out, and a balanced budget reduces public-debt accumulation, supporting long-run sustainability. Trade-off: short-run pain for long-run fiscal health.

★ Worked Example 2 — Multiplier-style (mirroring V22 PS1 / S25_RE PS1)

Setup: C = 200 + 0.75Yd, T = 200, G = 300, I = 300. Compute Y, C, Spriv, Spub, multiplier.

Y: Y = 200 + 0.75(Y − 200) + 300 + 300
    = 200 + 0.75Y − 150 + 300 + 300 = 650 + 0.75Y
0.25Y = 650 → Y = 2,600
C: C = 200 + 0.75(2600 − 200) = 200 + 0.75·2400 = 200 + 1,800 = 2,000
Spriv: Y − T − C = 2600 − 200 − 2,000 = 400
Spub: T − G = 200 − 300 = −100 (deficit)
Government multiplier: 1/(1 − 0.75) = 4. Meaning: ΔG = 100 → ΔY = 400.

What to say in writing: "The multiplier is 4 because each round of spending creates 75% of itself in induced consumption: 100 → 75 → 56.25 → … which sums to 400. Public saving is negative (T < G), indicating a budget deficit; national saving S = Spriv + Spub = 400 − 100 = 300 = I (consistency check holds)."

★ Worked Example 3 — Open Keynesian Cross (mirroring S25_RE PS1)

Setup: Open economy, Y = C + I + G + NX. C = 100 + 0.8Yd, I = 200, T = 100, G = 200, NX = 50 − 0.1Y. Find Y, multiplier.

Y = 100 + 0.8(Y − 100) + 200 + 200 + 50 − 0.1Y
Y = 100 + 0.8Y − 80 + 200 + 200 + 50 − 0.1Y
Y − 0.7Y = 470
0.3Y = 470 → Y = 1,566.7
Open-economy multiplier: 1/(1 − MPC + m) = 1/(1 − 0.8 + 0.1) = 1/0.3 ≈ 3.33
(smaller than closed because some income leaks to imports)

Note: Open-economy multiplier is always smaller because of the import leakage (m = marginal propensity to import). Mention this leakage explicitly in the answer.

Problem Set 3 (≈20%) — Open Economy / Mundell-Fleming CONCEPT-HEAVY

What to expect. Two countries comparison (one fixed ER, one floating ER). Sub-questions: (a) define fixed vs floating; (b) effect of fiscal/monetary policy under one regime; (c) effect of tariffs (or capital-flow shock) under the other; (d) explain a curve diagram. No numerical computation in recent exams — pure concept/diagram. Tariffs/quotas appeared in S25 and V23 — high probability F26.

3.1 Exchange Rate Regimes

RegimeHow it worksExamples
FixedCentral bank pegs the exchange rate (e.g., to euro). Buys/sells reserves to keep e constant. Money supply (M) becomes endogenous.Denmark (DKK pegged to EUR)
FloatingExchange rate is set by market forces. M is exogenously controlled by the central bank.Germany within euro area (vs USD), USA, UK

3.2 The Mundell-Fleming Diagram (e on y-axis, Y on x-axis)

With perfect capital mobility, domestic interest rate r = world interest rate r* (otherwise capital floods in or out instantly). Two curves:

  • IS*: Y = C(Y−T) + I(r*) + G + NX(e) → downward-sloping (in (Y, e) plane). Higher e → lower NX → lower Y.
  • LM*: M/P = L(r*, Y). With r* fixed and M/P fixed, LM* is vertical at a unique Y.

3.3 The 4 Cases You MUST Memorize

RegimePolicyEffect on YEffect on eMechanism
FloatingExpansionary fiscal (↑G or ↓T)No changee appreciates (↑)IS* shifts right; with LM* vertical, Y is pinned. Higher r-pressure attracts capital → e ↑ → NX ↓ → cancels fiscal boost. Fiscal is INEFFECTIVE.
Expansionary monetary (↑M)Y ↑e depreciates (↓)LM* shifts right. Lower r-pressure pushes capital out → e ↓ → NX ↑ → boosts Y. Monetary is EFFECTIVE.
FixedExpansionary fiscal (↑G or ↓T)Y ↑e fixedIS* shifts right. To prevent appreciation, CB sells own currency → M ↑ → LM* shifts right alongside, full multiplier effect. Fiscal is EFFECTIVE.
Expansionary monetary (↑M)No changee fixed↑M would depreciate e → CB buys back currency, undoing ΔM. Monetary is INEFFECTIVE.
Memory hook: "Floating gives monetary; Fixed gives fiscal." This single sentence is the core of Mundell-Fleming.

3.4 Trade Policy (tariffs/quotas) — appeared 2× recent

Tariffs make imports more expensive → ↓M → ↑NX (at any given e). The IS* curve shifts right.

RegimeEffect on YEffect on eEffect on NX
FloatingNo change (LM* vertical)e appreciatesNX unchanged in equilibrium (e adjustment offsets the tariff)
FixedY ↑e fixedNX ↑ (CB intervenes to prevent appreciation, M ↑, Y ↑)
Subtle point for an A: Even under fixed ER, while NX increases in equilibrium, this comes at the cost of higher M (inflation risk) and reserve accumulation. Mention this nuance.

3.5 Why NX is decreasing in e

If domestic currency appreciates (e ↑): foreign goods become relatively cheaper for domestic consumers (↑M), and domestic goods become more expensive abroad (↓X). So X − M = NX falls. This is why the IS* curve in (e, Y) space slopes down.

3.6 Interest-rate / aggregate demand decomposition (S25 PS3 d)

Given Y = C + I + G with C = f(Y − T), I = f(r), G fixed: an interest-rate increase has two channels:

  • Direct: ↑r → ↓I (investment is the most rate-sensitive component).
  • Indirect: Lower I → lower Y → lower disposable income → lower C.
  • Net: Y falls. Both I and C fall; G unchanged.

Problem Set 4 (≈20%) — Solow Growth Model NUMERICAL

What to expect. Two countries (or one country, two periods). Production function y = k1/2 (square-root) appeared in 11/12 past exams. Modern exams (V23+) include population growth (n) AND technology (g) in the equation. You compute steady-state {k*, y*, sy*, c*} for each country/period, then explain golden rule. Always asked: "Is the steady-state level always at golden rule?" Answer: NO.

4.1 The Setup

y = f(k) = k1/2  (per worker)
sf(k) = s·k1/2 = saving per worker
Break-even investment per worker: (δ + n + g)·k
Δk = s·k1/2 − (δ + n + g)·k

4.2 Steady-State Recipe (you'll do this in 60 seconds with practice)

At steady state, Δk = 0:

s·k*1/2 = (δ + n + g)·k*
Divide both sides by k*1/2:
s = (δ + n + g)·k*1/2
k*1/2 = s / (δ + n + g)   ⇒   k* = [s / (δ + n + g)]²

Once you have k*, the rest are direct:

QuantityFormula
Steady-state output per workery* = √k*
Steady-state saving per workersy* = s·y*
Steady-state consumption per workerc* = (1 − s)·y* = y* − sy*

4.3 Worked Example — Mirroring S25 PS4

Country A: s = 0.15, n = 0.04, δ = 0.03, g = 0.02. Country B: s = 0.20, n = 0.04, δ = 0.03, g = 0.04.

Country A: δ + n + g = 0.03 + 0.04 + 0.02 = 0.09

k*A = (0.15/0.09)² = (1.667)² ≈ 2.78
y*A = √2.78 ≈ 1.667
sy*A = 0.15 · 1.667 ≈ 0.250
c*A = 0.85 · 1.667 ≈ 1.417

Country B: δ + n + g = 0.03 + 0.04 + 0.04 = 0.11

k*B = (0.20/0.11)² = (1.818)² ≈ 3.31
y*B = √3.31 ≈ 1.818
sy*B = 0.20 · 1.818 ≈ 0.364
c*B = 0.80 · 1.818 ≈ 1.455

Discussion (always asked): "Country B has a higher saving rate (0.20 vs 0.15) which raises k* and y*. But B also has a higher technology growth rate g (0.04 vs 0.02), which raises the break-even investment requirement. The net effect is that B has higher k*, y*, and consumption per worker than A — its higher saving outweighs its higher (δ+n+g). Both countries grow at rates n+g (population + technology), but B's level of output per worker is higher in steady state."

4.4 Golden Rule (concept question — always asked)

The golden rule level of capital is the k that maximises consumption per worker in steady state. At that point, MPK = δ + n + g.

Consumption per worker: c* = f(k*) − (δ + n + g)·k*
∂c*/∂k = f'(k) − (δ + n + g) = 0
Golden rule condition: MPK(kgold) = δ + n + g

Is the steady-state always at the golden rule? NO.

  • The actual steady-state k* depends on the saving rate s (chosen by households / policy).
  • The golden-rule kgold is fixed by technology (when MPK = δ+n+g).
  • Equality holds only by coincidence — for a specific s = sgold.
  • If s < sgold: under-saving. Raising s increases c* eventually.
  • If s > sgold: over-saving. Lowering s increases c*.
Mankiw's "MPK − δ vs n + g" version (advanced): Some Mankiw editions phrase the golden rule as MPK − δ = n + g. Same condition rearranged. Use whichever form your slides showed.

4.5 Comparative-statics summary

ChangeEffect on k*Effect on y*Effect on growth rate of Y
↑ s (saving rate)↑↑No change in long-run rate (only level)
↑ n (pop growth)↓↓↑ (per-capita stays at g, but Y grows at n+g)
↑ δ (depreciation)↓↓No change in long-run rate
↑ g (technology)↓ in level form, but per-effective-workerper-worker grows at g↑ (the only source of long-run per-capita growth!)
Key insight Mankiw stresses: Only technology growth (g) drives long-run growth in output per capita. Capital accumulation alone can't sustain it because of diminishing returns.

Problem Set 5 (≈20%) — Consumption + CPI/Data MIXED

What to expect. Two parts: (1) compare consumption theories — Keynes vs Friedman PIH OR Keynes vs Modigliani LCH (50/50 chance). (2) Real-world data interpretation — Danish CPI table (commodity groups) with %-change calculations and short-answer interpretation.

5.1 Three Consumption Theories

TheoryEquationKey ideaAPC behavior
Keynes (Absolute Income Hypothesis, 1936)C = a + b·Yd, b = MPC ∈ (0,1)Current disposable income drives consumption. There is a fixed autonomous component (a). Short-run focus.APC = C/Y = a/Y + b → APC falls as Y rises.
Friedman PIH (Permanent Income Hypothesis, 1957)C = α·YP, where YP = average expected long-run incomeHouseholds smooth consumption based on permanent income. Transitory income shocks are saved/borrowed-against, not spent.APC roughly constant in the long run, varies with YT/Y in short run.
Modigliani LCH (Life-Cycle Hypothesis, 1954)C = (W + R·Y)/T
W = wealth, R = retirement years left, T = remaining life		Consumers smooth consumption over a finite life. Save during working years, dissave in retirement.APC depends on age and W/Y ratio. Predicts hump-shaped saving.

5.2 How to Answer "Compare X vs Keynes"

Structure your answer in 3 paragraphs:

  1. State Keynes: "Keynes (1936) proposed that current disposable income is the main determinant of consumption. C = a + b·Yd with 0 < b < 1. APC = a/Y + b falls as income rises, predicting a higher saving rate at higher incomes."
  2. State X: "Friedman/PIH (or Modigliani/LCH) argued that consumers look beyond current income. Friedman defined permanent income as average expected long-run income; transitory income shocks are smoothed by saving or borrowing. Modigliani's LCH stresses smoothing over the full lifetime — saving in working years, dissaving in retirement."
  3. Differences in implications: "The key difference is whether transitory income changes affect consumption. Keynes says yes (proportional to MPC); Friedman/Modigliani say no (or only minimally). This explains why APC stays roughly constant in the long run (a puzzle for Keynes) but falls in cross-section (consistent with Keynes if higher-income households have a larger transitory share)."
For an A: Mention the Kuznets puzzle — empirically, APC is constant in long-run time series but falls in cross-section. Keynes can't explain this; Friedman and Modigliani both can. This piece of evidence is what motivated the newer theories.

5.3 The CPI Table Calculation (S25 / V23_RE pattern)

You'll be given a table like Statistics Denmark's CPI by commodity group. Sub-questions:

  1. "Which category showed the most fluctuation?" → Compute max − min across the 4 months for each row, identify the largest range.
  2. "Why did Education stay flat?" → Education prices are largely set by the public sector and tuition is regulated → low responsiveness to market shocks.
  3. "Why is Restaurants & Hotels volatile?" → Labour-intensive (wage shocks transmit), seasonal demand, energy/food input cost pass-through, optional spending so demand is more elastic.
  4. "How would inflation in essentials affect consumption?" → Use Y = C + I + G. Real disposable income falls → C falls (essentials inelastic, so households cut discretionary). Lower C lowers AD, lowers Y. Possible second-round: lower investment, central-bank tightening.

5.4 %-change Formula (will be needed)

%Δ = (new value − old value) / old value × 100
e.g., CPI rises from 105 to 108: %Δ = (108 − 105)/105 × 100 ≈ 2.86%

5.5 Connecting Data to Y = C + I + G

If asked how a CPI shock or inflation episode affects the macroeconomy, walk through:

  • ↑P (essentials) → ↓real disposable income → ↓C (Keynesian channel) → ↓AD → ↓Y
  • If central bank tightens (↑r) → ↓I → further ↓Y
  • Government may compensate (↑G) but that's a policy choice, not automatic
  • NX impact via real exchange rate: domestic inflation → real appreciation → ↓NX (further drag on Y)

Bonus Topics (for extra-mark questions)

B.1 Cobb-Douglas Production Function

Although the new exam format (V23+) doesn't make Cobb-Douglas a primary problem, it can appear in PS3 or PS5. Master the basics in 5 minutes:

Y = A·Lα·Kβ, with α + β = 1 (typical CRS case)
MPL = ∂Y/∂L = α·A·Lα−1·Kβ = α·Y/L
MPK = ∂Y/∂K = β·A·Lα·Kβ−1 = β·Y/K
Real wage = MPL = αY/L  |  Real rent = MPK = βY/K
Total labor income = αY (constant share α)  |  Total capital income = βY

Numerical setup (V22 PS2 style): Y = L0.5K0.5, L = K = 1000 → Y = √(1000·1000) = 1000. MPL = 0.5·1000/1000 = 0.5. Labor income = 0.5·1000 = 500.

B.2 AD-AS and Supply Shocks

Three curves:

  • AD: derived from IS-LM; downward-sloping in (Y, P) space.
  • SRAS: upward-sloping (sticky-price model) — firms set prices, then output adjusts to demand.
  • LRAS: vertical at natural output Y̅ — long-run output is determined by factors of production, not P.

Adverse supply shock (e.g., oil price spike): SRAS shifts left/up. Short-run: P↑, Y↓ (stagflation). Long-run: economy returns to Y̅ as expected prices adjust. Policy: a central-bank monetary expansion can offset the demand side but worsens inflation; a fiscal targeted to lower input costs can help.

B.3 Phillips Curve in Detail

π = πe − β(u − un) + ν
π = inflation, πe = expected inflation, u = unemployment, un = natural rate, ν = supply shock

Trade-off: only short-run. To reduce π, must accept u > un (sacrifice ratio). Long-run: πe adjusts, Phillips curve shifts, no permanent trade-off (vertical at un).

B.4 Quantity Theory of Money & Fisher

M·V = P·Y → in growth rates: %ΔM + %ΔV = %ΔP + %ΔY
Assuming V constant: %ΔP = %ΔM − %ΔY (inflation = money growth − real growth)
Fisher: i = r + πe; Real interest rate r = i − πe

Money neutrality (long run): ΔM affects only nominal variables (P, i), not real (Y, r, employment). This is the classical dichotomy.

B.6 GDP Concepts (for T/F or short-answer)

GDP = Total value of final goods/services produced within a country
GNP = GDP + net factor income from abroad
NNP = GNP − depreciation
Nominal GDP at current prices  |  Real GDP at base-year prices
GDP deflator = (Nominal/Real)·100 = Paasche index
CPI = (cost of base-year basket at current prices)/(cost at base prices)·100 = Laspeyres index

9.1 Cobb-Douglas Numerical Example (V20 PS2 / V22 PS2 style)

Cobb-Douglas appeared in PS2 of seven older exams (V20, V20_RE, V22, V22_RE etc.). Format: given Y = A·Kα·L(1−α), compute MPL, MPK, factor shares.

★ Worked Example A — V20 PS2

Setup: Y = A·Kα·L(1−α), with α = 0.6, K = 25, L = 50, A = 1. Compute MPL, MPK, total labour income, total capital income.

Step 1 — compute Y:

Y = 1 · 250.6 · 500.4
250.6 = e0.6·ln25 = e0.6·3.2189 = e1.9313 ≈ 6.899
500.4 = e0.4·ln50 = e0.4·3.9120 = e1.5648 ≈ 4.782
Y ≈ 6.899 · 4.782 ≈ 33.0

Step 2 — MPL = (1−α)·Y/L = 0.4·Y/L:

MPL = 0.4 · 33.0 / 50 = 0.264

Step 3 — MPK = α·Y/K = 0.6·Y/K:

MPK = 0.6 · 33.0 / 25 = 0.792

Step 4 — Income shares (classical theory says factors paid their MP):

Labour income = MPL · L = 0.264 · 50 = 13.2  (= (1−α)·Y = 0.4·33 = 13.2 ✓)
Capital income = MPK · K = 0.792 · 25 = 19.8  (= α·Y = 0.6·33 = 19.8 ✓)
Total = 13.2 + 19.8 = 33 = Y ✓ (Euler's theorem holds for CRS)

Why are factors paid their MP? — classical theory answer: "In competitive markets, profit-maximising firms hire labour until the additional revenue from one more unit of labour (P·MPL) equals its cost (W). So W/P = MPL. Same for capital: R/P = MPK. Under constant returns to scale (α + (1−α) = 1), Euler's theorem guarantees that paying each factor its MP exactly exhausts total output (no leftover profit)."

Shortcut: If α + β = 1, you don't even need to compute Y to get the shares. Labour share = (1−α) ALWAYS regardless of K, L, A. Capital share = α ALWAYS. So if α = 0.6, labour gets 40% of Y and capital gets 60% — full stop.

9.2 Fisher Equation & CPI-Based Inflation (V19 PS1)

Asks: given nominal interest rate i and a CPI change, compute the implied real interest rate.

★ Worked Example B — V19 PS1

Setup: Nominal rate i = 3%. CPI rises from 125 to 126. Find the real interest rate r.

Inflation π = (126 − 125)/125 · 100 = 0.8%
Fisher equation: i = r + π
r = i − π = 3% − 0.8% = 2.2%

Interpretation: The real return on saving is only 2.2% — the nominal 3% return is partially eroded by 0.8% inflation. If inflation rises further while i stays fixed, the real interest rate falls (worse for savers, better for borrowers).

Opportunity cost of holding money in liquid form: The interest you forgo by not investing it. If you hold 1,000 DKK as cash, you lose 1,000 · i = 30 DKK per year (or in real terms, 22 DKK per year after adjusting for inflation).

9.3 QTM Numerical Calculations (V19_RE PS1, V20 PS2c)

Quantity Theory of Money: M·V = P·Y. Take logs and differentiate to get growth-rate form: %ΔM + %ΔV = %ΔP + %ΔY.

★ Worked Example C — Inflation from money growth

Setup: Money supply grows at 50% per year. Real GDP grows at 3% per year. Velocity is constant. What is the inflation rate implied by QTM?

%ΔM + %ΔV = %ΔP + %ΔY
50% + 0% = π + 3%
π = 47% (hyperinflation territory)

How is inflation determined according to QTM? QTM says inflation = money growth rate − real output growth rate (when V is constant). In long-run / classical view: real Y is determined by factors of production (K, L, A), independent of M. So any extra money growth feeds 1-for-1 into prices. This is "money neutrality": ΔM does not affect real Y, only nominal P.

9.4 AD-AS Supply Shock (V20 PS3, V22 PS3 — COVID / Russia-Ukraine)

Three curves in (P, Y) space:

  • AD: downward-sloping. Higher P → lower real money supply → higher r → lower I → lower Y.
  • SRAS: upward-sloping (or horizontal in sticky-price form). Firms keep prices fixed short-run, output adjusts to demand.
  • LRAS: vertical at natural output Y̅. Long-run output is determined by factor inputs (K, L, A), not by P.

Adverse supply shock (oil crisis, pandemic, war):

Short-run effect: SRAS shifts upward/leftward (production becomes more expensive at every level of Y).

VariableEffect SREffect LR (after adjustment)
Output Y↓ (recession)Returns to Y̅ as wages/prices adjust
Price level P↑ (stagflation)Stays elevated — supply shock leaves a permanent price level shift
Unemployment u↑ (above un)Returns to un
Natural rate Y̅Unchanged (LRAS doesn't shift unless capital/labour change)Same

Policy responses:

PolicyEffectTrade-off
Expansionary monetary (↑M)AD shifts right → Y rises back toward Y̅But P rises further — accommodating the shock means higher long-run inflation.
Expansionary fiscal (↑G or ↓T)AD shifts right → similar effectSame problem — output recovery at the cost of more inflation, plus higher public debt.
Do nothing (let SRAS adjust back over time)P falls back, Y rises back to Y̅Slow — recession persists during the adjustment period.
Supply-side (e.g., subsidise input costs)Tackles the shock directly: SRAS shifts back rightward.Best response if achievable; expensive and slow.
Standard answer for the policy question: "An expansionary monetary policy (↑M) shifts AD right, raising Y back toward Y̅. The trade-off is that this accelerates inflation. Alternatively, accommodating supply-side relief (input subsidies, energy support) attacks the SRAS shift directly without compounding inflation, but it's slower to implement."

9.5 Open Keynesian Cross with NX(ε) (V19 PS2, V21_RE A3, V22_RE PS1)

The open-economy Keynesian cross adds NX as a function of the real exchange rate ε:

Y = C + I + G + NX(ε),    NX = a − b·ε  (b > 0)
↑ε (real appreciation) → ↓NX → lower Y at any given r.
Open multiplier = 1/(1 − MPC + m), where m = marginal propensity to import (added leakage).

★ Worked Example D — V19 PS2 setup

Setup: Y = C + I + G + NX, with C = 250 + 0.75(Y − T), T = 1000, G = 1250, I = 450 − 25r (r* = 8), NX = 1000 − 125ε. Initial ε = 4.

Step 1 — find Y at ε = 4:

NX = 1000 − 125·4 = 500
I = 450 − 25·8 = 250
Y = 250 + 0.75(Y − 1000) + 250 + 1250 + 500
Y = 250 + 0.75Y − 750 + 250 + 1250 + 500 = 0.75Y + 1500
0.25Y = 1500 → Y = 6,000

Step 2 — what if ε rises to 5?

NX = 1000 − 125·5 = 375 (NX falls by 125 due to appreciation)
Y = 250 + 0.75(Y − 1000) + 250 + 1250 + 375
0.25Y = 1375 → Y = 5,500 (drops by 500)

Why does Y drop by exactly 500 when NX drops by 125? Multiplier effect: ΔY = ΔNX/(1 − MPC) = 125/0.25 = 500. ✓

Private and public savings at ε = 4:

C = 250 + 0.75·(6000 − 1000) = 250 + 3750 = 4,000
Spriv = Y − T − C = 6000 − 1000 − 4000 = 1,000
Spub = T − G = 1000 − 1250 = −250
National saving = Spriv + Spub = 750 = I + NX = 250 + 500 = 750 ✓

9.6 Balance of Payments / S − I = NX (V21 A1)

The accounting identity for an open economy:

S − I = NX  (national saving minus investment = net exports = current account)

Interpretation:

  • If S > I (Denmark's typical case): the country saves more than it invests at home. The excess flows abroad as net foreign lending → trade surplus (NX > 0). Capital outflow = current-account surplus.
  • If S < I: country imports capital to fund excess investment → trade deficit (NX < 0). Capital inflow = current-account deficit.

What factors influence NX?

  • Real exchange rate ε (↑ε → ↓NX).
  • Domestic income Y (↑Y → ↑imports → ↓NX).
  • Foreign income Y* (↑Y* → ↑foreign demand for our exports → ↑NX).
  • Trade policy (tariffs, quotas).
  • Domestic vs foreign price levels (terms of trade).

How does this relate to S and I? If Denmark runs a sustained trade surplus (NX > 0), it's accumulating claims on foreigners (higher net foreign assets). This means national saving exceeds domestic investment — capital is flowing out to be invested abroad where returns are higher. In Mundell-Fleming with perfect capital mobility under fixed ER, this surplus is what allows the central bank to hold reserves stable.

9.7 Short-Answer Concept Bank (V21 A2 style — 5 micro-questions)

QuestionAnswer
Difference between exogenous and endogenous variables? Exogenous = determined outside the model, taken as given (e.g., G, T, M in IS-LM; population in Solow). Endogenous = determined inside the model (e.g., Y, r, P in IS-LM). Models explain endogenous variables as functions of exogenous ones.
Business cycles and Okun's law? Real GDP fluctuates above/below potential during business cycles. Okun's law: a 1% rise in unemployment is associated with roughly a 2% drop in GDP relative to potential. So during expansions (Y above Y̅), unemployment falls below un; during recessions, unemployment rises above un. The link is mechanical: less output = fewer workers needed.
Phillips curve link to inflation in business cycles? Phillips: π = πe − β(u − un) + ν. In expansion (u < un), inflation rises above expectations. In recession (u > un), inflation falls. So business cycles produce procyclical inflation — booms cause inflation, busts cause disinflation. Long run: πe adjusts, no permanent trade-off.
Keynes vs Friedman PIH (short vs long term)? Short-run Keynes: consumption tracks current disposable income; transitory income shocks raise C by MPC×ΔYT. Friedman PIH: consumption depends on permanent income only — transitory shocks barely affect C (saved/borrowed against). Long-run: Keynes predicts APC falls as income rises (cross-section evidence supports), but in long-run time series APC is constant — only PIH explains this Kuznets puzzle.
How do future expectations affect consumption? Expectations of higher future income raise C today (consumption smoothing). PIH and LCH both say: forward-looking households spread expected lifetime income evenly. So an expected raise leads to higher current C even before the income arrives — borrowing or reduced saving. Keynes ignores expectations: only current Yd matters. Empirically expectations matter, supporting PIH/LCH.

9.8 Mundell-Fleming Policy Choice Drill (V21 A3)

You may be asked to recommend a policy from a menu. The reasoning matters more than the calculation.

★ Worked Example E — V21 A3 setup (small open, fixed ER)

Setup: Small open economy, fixed ER. C = 0.95(Y−T), I = 250 − 10r* with r = r* = 5, G = 200, T = 200, NX = 50.

Initial equilibrium:

I = 250 − 10·5 = 200
Y = 0.95(Y − 200) + 200 + 200 + 50 = 0.95Y − 190 + 450 = 0.95Y + 260
0.05Y = 260 → Y = 5,200

Three policy options the government considers:

OptionEffect on YWhy
(a) ↑G to 250 (without ↑T)Y rises (under fixed ER, fiscal is effective)New Y: 0.05Y = 260 + 50 → Y = 6,200. ΔY = 1,000. Multiplier = 1/(1−0.95) = 20.
(b) ↓r to 4% via monetary policyY unchanged (under fixed ER, monetary is ineffective)Cannot independently set r ≠ r* under fixed ER + perfect capital mobility. CB defends e at the cost of M endogenously adjusting. So r stays at r*.
(c) ↑NX to 75 via trade policy (e.g. tariff)Y rises (under fixed ER, trade boost is effective)0.05Y = 260 + 25 → Y = 5,700. ΔY = 500. Smaller boost than fiscal because ΔNX = 25 < ΔG = 50.

Recommendation: Option (a) gives the largest output boost. Option (b) is mechanically impossible under fixed ER. Option (c) works but is smaller. So if the government wants maximum employment effect: raise G. Caveat: this widens the budget deficit and may cause real appreciation pressure that the central bank must offset by selling reserves.

Shifting from fixed to floating (no calculations needed): Under floating, monetary policy becomes powerful (option b would now work — ↓r → ↓e → ↑NX → ↑Y). Fiscal policy becomes ineffective (↑G → IS* shifts but Y is pinned by vertical LM*; e appreciates, NX falls, cancelling the fiscal boost). So policy effectiveness completely flips.

9.9 Solow with Shifted Production Function (V21 A4: y = 3·k1/2)

Some exams add a scale factor in front of the production function. The recipe is the same, but you must keep track of the constant.

★ Worked Example F — V21 A4 setup

Setup: y = 3·k1/2, sf(k) = 0.24·f(k) (so s = 0.24), δ = 0.12, no n or g.

Steady state (Δk = 0):

sf(k*) = δ·k*
0.24·(3·k*1/2) = 0.12·k*
0.72·k*1/2 = 0.12·k*
0.72/0.12 = k*1/2 = 6
k* = 36
y* = 3·√36 = 3·6 = 18
Saving per worker = sy* = 0.24·18 = 4.32
Consumption per worker c* = (1 − s)·y* = 0.76·18 = 13.68

Steady-state level vs golden rule: The steady state is wherever investment exactly replaces depreciation; it's determined by s, δ (and n, g if present). The golden rule is the specific k that maximises consumption per worker — not the same as the steady state in general. The condition is MPK = δ (here, 1.5/k1/2 = 0.12 → k1/2 = 12.5 → kgold = 156.25, much higher than k* = 36). So this country is under-saving: raising s would increase steady-state consumption.

9.10 Multiple-Choice Style Question (V19 PS3 — trade-surplus identity)

This is the only "select correct" question I found in older exams. Worth knowing as it tests an identity that's easy to confuse.

Question (V19 PS3 Q1):

"When an economy is facing a trade surplus (X > IM), which is true and why?"

  1. A trade surplus is not good for the economy. Trade is better when X = IM.
  2. The demand for goods in the domestic economy = total demand for domestic goods.
  3. The demand for goods in the domestic economy > total demand for domestic goods.
  4. The demand for goods in the domestic economy < total demand for domestic goods.

Correct answer: D

Why:

  • Demand for goods in the domestic economy = C + I + G (what residents buy from anywhere — domestic + imports).
  • Demand for domestic goods = (C + I + G) − IM + X = C + I + G + NX (what gets purchased from domestic producers, including foreign demand).
  • If trade surplus → NX > 0 → demand for domestic goods > demand in domestic economy. So the domestic economy demands less than what's produced and sold to domestic + foreign buyers combined. Answer D.
  • (A) is normative and contestable — generally false in standard theory; balance isn't inherently optimal. (B) is only true if NX = 0. (C) reverses the relationship.

9.11 Phillips Curve Numerical (V19 PS3 Q2)

Given an adaptive Phillips curve where natural unemployment depends on past unemployment.

πt = πt−1 − 0.6(ut − un)
un = 0.25(ut−1 + ut−2 + ut−3 + ut−4)

Why does un depend on past u? This is the hysteresis idea: prolonged unemployment can permanently raise the natural rate (skill loss, discouraged workers, structural shifts). So if the economy stays in a recession for a while, un drifts upward — the Phillips trade-off shifts.

Effect of a shock at year t−4: A small rise in ut−4 raises the average un. With ut fixed, (ut − un) becomes smaller, so πt is higher. So a long-ago unemployment shock raises today's inflation through hysteresis-driven un.

Exam Tactics (4-hour written exam, all aids allowed)

Time allocation (for 240 minutes)

General rules

  • Always show working. Even if your final answer is wrong, partial credit is huge in this exam style.
  • Define terms before using them. "The IS curve represents…" — graders want to see you explain the concept, not just state numbers.
  • Write the formula first, then substitute. Don't skip steps.
  • Sanity-check. If MPC > 1, you've made an error. If multiplier is negative, error. If Y is negative, error.
  • Diagrams are gold. Always sketch IS-LM, AD-AS, or Solow when relevant. Label axes (Y on x; r or e or P on y) and curves clearly.
  • For T/F: answer T or F first, then 1 sentence. Don't write a paragraph.
  • For "in your opinion" questions: still anchor in economic theory — don't just state opinion. Use the framework Y = C + I + G or relevant model.
  • Open aids: Bring this document plus the cheat sheet, plus your textbook. Don't waste exam time looking up things you can memorize.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Confusing CPI weights (fixed) vs GDP deflator weights (current).
  • Confusing Okun's law sign (employment ↔ Y is positive; unemployment ↔ Y is negative).
  • Forgetting MPC ∈ (0, 1) — leads to absurd multipliers.
  • In Mundell-Fleming, drawing IS* as vertical (it's LM* that's vertical).
  • In Solow, forgetting to include all of (δ + n + g) in the steady-state denominator.
  • For golden rule, saying steady-state IS golden rule (it's only when s = sgold).
