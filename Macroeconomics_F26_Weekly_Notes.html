Week 6 — Intro, GDP, CPI, classical national income Mankiw Ch. 1–4

What is macroeconomics, really?

Microeconomics looks at one market — apples, jeans, software developers. Macroeconomics zooms out to the whole economy at once: total output, total employment, prices in general, interest rates. Instead of asking "what's the price of bread?" macro asks "are prices rising in general (inflation)?"

In plain English. Think of microeconomics as a magnifying glass on one tree, and macroeconomics as a drone view of the whole forest. Both are valid — they just answer different questions. Macro doesn't care about individual firms; it cares about jobs, GDP, inflation, and the central bank — the things on the news.
Three central questions of macro:
1. Why do some countries get rich while others stay poor? (growth)
2. Why does the economy go through booms and busts? (business cycles)
3. What can the government do to keep things stable? (policy)

Models, exogenous, endogenous

An economic model is a simplified picture — usually a few equations and a graph. We feed it some inputs and the model tells us the outputs.

  • Exogenous variables = things determined outside the model. We just take them as given. Examples: government spending G, the money supply M, the world interest rate r*.
  • Endogenous variables = things determined inside the model. The model explains them. Examples: GDP (Y), the interest rate r, the price level P.

Think of a recipe: ingredients are exogenous (you put them in), the cake is endogenous (the recipe produces it).

In plain English. "Exogenous" just means "decided OUTSIDE the model — we don't try to explain WHY it's that value, we just take it as given." "Endogenous" means "the model figures it out for us." When an exam says "the government raises G", G is exogenous (a choice). The resulting Y and r are endogenous (the model predicts them).

GDP — Gross Domestic Product (Ch 2)

GDP = the total market value of all final goods and services produced inside a country in a given period (usually a year or quarter).

In plain English. GDP is the country's "report card" for the year — how much total economic activity happened inside its borders. It's the single most-used measure of an economy's size. "Final" means we count the finished product (a loaf of bread), not the wheat and the flour separately — otherwise we'd be double-counting.
Three ways to measure GDP — they all give the same number:
1. Production approach: add up the value of everything produced (avoiding double-counting by using only final goods, or summing value-added at each stage).
2. Expenditure approach: GDP = C + I + G + NX  (C = consumption, I = investment, G = government spending, NX = net exports = exports − imports).
3. Income approach: add up all wages, profits, rents, interest paid in the economy.

Nominal vs Real GDP

DefinitionWhy we care
Nominal GDPOutput valued at current pricesMixes up real growth with inflation — misleading for comparing years.
Real GDPOutput valued at base-year (constant) pricesStrips out inflation, so changes reflect actual production changes.
In plain English. If your salary doubles in a year, but prices also double, are you actually better off? No. Nominal GDP is like that doubled salary — it includes both real growth AND price inflation. Real GDP strips out the inflation so we can compare apples to apples across years. If real GDP rose 3%, that's actual extra stuff produced. If nominal GDP rose 8% and inflation was 5%, real growth was only 3%.
Example. In 2024 the country produced 100 cars at €20,000 each → nominal GDP = 2,000,000. In 2025 the same 100 cars are made but the price rose to €25,000 → nominal GDP = 2,500,000 (looks like 25% growth). Real GDP using 2024 prices = 100 × 20,000 = 2,000,000 in BOTH years — actual production didn't change. The 25% is purely inflation.

GDP Deflator

GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100
Nominal GDP = output at current prices  |  Real GDP = output at constant base-year prices

It's the price index implied by GDP. Uses current quantities as weights — economists call this a Paasche index.

In plain English. If you divide a salary doubled by inflation (nominal) by the actual purchasing-power equivalent (real), you get the inflation factor itself. That's what the deflator measures — the price level "baked into" GDP. If deflator = 110 with base year = 100, prices have risen 10% since the base year.

CPI — Consumer Price Index

The CPI measures the cost of a fixed basket of consumer goods, year by year. Statistics Denmark picks a representative basket once and then tracks its cost over time. Uses fixed weights — a Laspeyres index.

CPI = (Cost of basket at current prices / Cost of basket at base prices) × 100
In plain English. Imagine a "typical household's shopping cart" — bread, eggs, rent, electricity, etc. — fixed in 2015 (the base year). The CPI says "if you bought that same cart in 2025, how much more would it cost than in 2015?" If the cart cost €1000 in 2015 and €1200 in 2025, CPI = 120 (20% more expensive). The "fixed weights" part means the basket stays the same — they don't update what's in it every year.
CPI vs Deflator — the trap question: CPI uses fixed weights; the GDP deflator uses current quantities. The exam loves to reverse this.

Inflation

π = (CPIt − CPIt−1) / CPIt−1 × 100
π (Greek letter "pi") = inflation rate  |  CPIt = price index this period  |  CPIt−1 = price index last period

If CPI rises from 105 to 108, inflation = 3/105 ≈ 2.86%.

CPI %-change technique — recurring in PS5

The exam often gives a CPI table and asks for month-over-month or year-over-year %-changes. Here's the technique:

%Δ = (new value − old value) / old value × 100

Other useful aggregates

  • GNP = GDP + net income from abroad. (What citizens earn, regardless of where they work.)
  • NNP = GNP − depreciation. (What's left after replacing worn-out capital.)
  • Disposable income (Yd) = Y − T. What households actually have to spend.

Classical Theory of National Income (Ch 3)

This is the long-run view: prices are flexible, markets clear, everything is determined by real factors.

The production function

Y = F(K, L) — output depends on capital and labour.
Y = total output  |  K = capital stock (machines, factories)  |  L = labour (workers)

Standard assumption: constant returns to scale (CRS). Doubling both K and L doubles Y. The Cobb-Douglas form is most common: Y = A·Kα·L(1−α)  (A = technology, α (alpha) = capital's share of income, 1−α = labour's share).

Returns-to-Scale diagnostic (T/F exam favourite — appears in 5+ exams). Take any production function. Multiply ALL inputs by some factor t > 1 (e.g. double them, t=2) and ask: does Y rise by more, exactly, or less than t?
  • IRS (Increasing RTS): Doubling inputs MORE than doubles output (e.g. 100 → 250 means 2.5× > 2× → IRS).
  • CRS (Constant RTS): Doubling inputs EXACTLY doubles output (e.g. 100 → 200).
  • DRS (Decreasing RTS): Doubling inputs raises output by LESS than double (e.g. 120 → 180 means 1.5× < 2× → DRS; or 5 → 7 means 1.4× → DRS).
Cobb-Douglas shortcut: For Y = A·Kα·Lβ, check α + β. If α+β = 1 → CRS. If > 1 → IRS. If < 1 → DRS. Trap: the exam often states a numerical example ("inputs doubled, Y went from 120 to 180") that LOOKS like IRS but is actually DRS — always compute the ratio.
Cobb-Douglas exponent ordering warning. Most textbooks write Y = Kα·L(1−α) with α = capital share. But some exam problems flip it: Y = Lα·Kβ with α = labour share, or Y = A·Lα·Kβ. Read each problem carefully and ask "which exponent goes with which factor?" — the share each factor earns equals the exponent on that factor.

Worked partial derivatives (so you can derive MPL/MPK from scratch)

You don't always have to memorise shortcuts. Here's the full derivation for Y = Kα·L(1−α) (treating A = 1 for simplicity):

MPL = ∂Y/∂L = (1−α)·Kα·L−α = (1−α)·(K/L)α
MPK = ∂Y/∂K = α·Kα−1·L1−α = α·(L/K)1−α

Equivalent shortcut form: MPL = (1−α)·Y/L  |  MPK = α·Y/K
(Take the exponent on a factor, multiply by Y, divide by that factor.)
Worked example. Y = K0.5·L0.5 with K = L = 1,000. First Y = √1,000 × √1,000 = 1,000. Then MPL = 0.5·(1,000/1,000)0.5 = 0.5. Labour income = MPL × L = 0.5 × 1,000 = 500. MPK = 0.5 too, capital income = 500. Total = 1,000 = Y ✓ (Euler's theorem confirmed).

How factors get paid

In a competitive market, firms hire each factor up to the point where the value of one more unit equals what it costs:

  • Hire one more worker until real wage W/P = MPL (marginal product of labour). MPL = ∂Y/∂L.
  • Rent one more unit of capital until real rent R/P = MPK. MPK = ∂Y/∂K.
In plain English. A bakery owner thinks: "If I hire another baker, they'll produce €15/hour of extra bread. So I'd pay them anything up to €15/hour and still make money. If the market wage is €12, I'll hire — and so will every other bakery, pushing wages up until they hit €15." Same logic for machines: rent them until the rent equals what they produce. This is why wages reflect productivity.
Why? Plain-English logic. If hiring an extra worker produces 5 units of output (worth 5×P euros) but you only pay them W euros, you'd hire another. You keep hiring until the marginal worker's output exactly covers their wage — i.e., MPL = W/P. Same logic for capital.

Income shares

For Cobb-Douglas Y = Kα·L(1−α):

  • Labour income = MPL × L = (1−α) × Y. So labour gets share (1−α) of total output.
  • Capital income = MPK × K = α × Y. Capital gets share α.
  • Together they sum to Y exactly (Euler's theorem). No leftover, no shortage.
In plain English. The exponents in the Cobb-Douglas function are literally the shares of income each factor gets. If α = 0.3 (the typical empirical value), capital gets 30% of total income and labour gets 70%. This is one of macroeconomics' tidiest results — the math elegantly divides the entire economic pie between the two factors with nothing left over.

How total income gets divided up between C, I, G

In the classical model, output Y is fixed by the production function. The interest rate r adjusts so that saving = investment in equilibrium:

Sprivate = Y − T − C,   Spublic = T − G,   Snational = Sprivate + Spublic = Y − C − G
Equilibrium: S = I(r)
S = saving (private + public)  |  T = taxes  |  r = real interest rate  |  I(r) = investment as a function of r (decreasing)
What does this mean in plain English? Think of it like a giant pot of money. Households save some of their income → that's the supply of loanable funds. Firms want to borrow money to invest → that's the demand. The interest rate r is the "price" that makes the pot balance. If saving is high relative to demand, r falls. If borrowing demand is high, r rises.
The loanable funds market. Saving (vertical because Y is fixed in the long run) meets investment demand. The equilibrium real interest rate r* clears the market — the black dot sits exactly where the two lines cross.

If government spending rises (↑G), national saving falls (because Spub = T − G falls). The S curve shifts left, raising r and reducing I. This is crowding out — even in the classical model, government spending squeezes out private investment.

Crowding out — formal definition (used throughout the course). The reduction in private investment caused by government borrowing pushing up the real interest rate. Appears in three contexts: (1) classical loanable funds (here — S shifts left, r rises, I falls); (2) IS-LM short run (↑G shifts IS right, raises Y AND r; the higher r reduces I — partial offset); (3) Mundell-Fleming closed perspective (fiscal under floating ER is "fully crowded out" via the exchange-rate channel — the appreciation cancels NX one-for-one, leaving Y unchanged).

The Monetary System (Ch 4)

What money is and what it does

Money is anything that's a (1) medium of exchange, (2) store of value, (3) unit of account. Cash, bank deposits, etc.

In plain English. Money is just anything society agrees to accept as payment. Cash works. Bank deposits work. Gold used to. Bitcoin partly does. The 3 functions are: (1) you can spend it (medium of exchange), (2) it holds value over time (store of value), (3) prices are quoted in it (unit of account). When something fails any of these — like during hyperinflation when cash loses value daily — it stops being money.

Money supply (M)

Controlled by the central bank through several tools (open market operations, reserve requirements, the discount rate). For now we just take M as exogenous.

Quantity Theory of Money (QTM)

M × V = P × Y
M = money supply (controlled by central bank)  |  V = velocity (how often each money unit gets used per year)  |  P = price level  |  Y = real output
In growth-rate form: %ΔM + %ΔV = %ΔP + %ΔY
If V is constant: π = %ΔM − %ΔY  (inflation = money growth − real output growth)
In plain English. The "amount of money in circulation" (M) times "how fast it moves" (V) must equal the "total value of all transactions" (P × Y). If the central bank prints 10% more money but the economy only produces 2% more stuff, the extra 8% has to go somewhere — and it shows up as higher prices (8% inflation). This is why printing money causes inflation in the long run.
Plain-English meaning: If the central bank prints 10% more money but real output only grows 2%, prices rise by 8%. Money beyond what the economy actually produces just bids up prices.

The classical dichotomy and money neutrality

Classical economists separated real variables (Y, K, L, real wages) from nominal variables (P, M, nominal wages). In the long run, changes in M only affect nominal stuff — Y, employment, real wages stay the same. This is called money neutrality.

In plain English. Imagine doubling everyone's bank balance overnight. You'd FEEL twice as rich initially, but as everyone tries to spend, prices double too. Real purchasing power is unchanged. Production, employment, and your actual standard of living are unaffected — only the numbers got bigger. Money is "neutral" because it doesn't change real things in the long run. This is why printing money can't make a country genuinely richer.

Fisher Equation

i = r + πe    (nominal interest = real interest + expected inflation)
i = nominal interest rate (what you see on a bank statement)  |  r = real interest rate (purchasing-power return)  |  πe = expected inflation

If you're a lender, you want a real return r. If you also expect inflation of 3%, you'll demand i = r + 3% nominally so the real return is still r. Real interest rate r = i − π.

In plain English. If your savings account pays 5% but prices rise 3% a year, your money's purchasing power only grows 2%. That's the REAL return. The "5%" you see on the statement is the NOMINAL return — but it's misleading because inflation eats some of it. The Fisher equation just makes this explicit: real = nominal − inflation. Always think in real terms when comparing across time.
Exam check (Week 6) — Past exam question + worked solution:

From V19 PS1 (10%): "The Fisher equation states i = r + π. Assume the nominal interest rate is 3% and CPI is 125. The Central Bank's monetary policy raises CPI to 126. How will this affect the real interest rate?"
Step 1 — find inflation: π = (126 − 125)/125 × 100 = 0.8%.
Step 2 — apply Fisher: r = i − π = 3% − 0.8% = 2.2%.
Step 3 — interpret: The real return on saving is only 2.2%. The 3% nominal return is partially eroded by the 0.8% inflation. Inflation-adjusted purchasing power grows at 2.2%, not 3%.

Week 8 — Classical theory continued + inflation Mankiw Ch. 3–5

Week 8 deepens what we started in week 6: the classical model of how prices and output are set, plus a chapter on inflation in detail.

Loanable funds market (Ch 3 deep dive)

In classical theory, the interest rate r is set in the market for loanable funds. Saving is the supply, investment is the demand:

  • Savings supply: S = Y − C − G (vertical line if Y is fixed by production).
  • Investment demand: I = I(r), downward-sloping (lower r → cheaper to borrow → more I).

Equilibrium r is where S = I.

In plain English. Think of it like a marketplace for "borrowable money." Savers offer their money (supply), borrowers want money (demand). The price is the interest rate r. If lots of people save but no one wants to borrow, r drops. If everyone wants to borrow but nobody saves, r rises. Equilibrium is where the savings supply meets the investment demand — that's the going interest rate in the economy.

Government deficits and saving

Suppose ↑G with T unchanged → public saving (T − G) falls → national saving falls → S curve shifts left → r rises → I falls. This is the classical version of crowding out.

Inflation: causes, effects, costs (Ch 5)

The cause: long-run inflation = money growth (mostly)

In the long run, with V and Y stable, π ≈ %ΔM. So if Denmark's central bank grows the money supply at 8% per year while real output grows 2%, inflation will be about 6%.

In plain English. Friedman's famous claim: "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon." If a country has persistent high inflation, the central bank is printing money too fast — period. Short-run inflation can come from many sources (supply shocks, demand surges), but sustained, long-run inflation almost always traces back to monetary policy.

The Fisher effect

If money growth rises by 5 percentage points, expected inflation rises by 5 pp, and the nominal interest rate rises by 5 pp too. The real rate is unaffected. This is the Fisher effect.

Costs of expected inflation

  • Shoe-leather costs: people hold less cash to avoid inflation, which means more trips to the ATM (literally wearing out shoe leather).
  • Menu costs: firms have to keep changing prices.
  • Tax distortions: tax codes are usually written in nominal terms, so inflation distorts effective tax rates.
  • Confusion: harder to compare prices across years.
In plain English. Even predictable inflation isn't free. Workers and firms waste time managing cash flow ("shoe-leather"), restaurants reprint menus more often ("menu costs"), tax brackets that aren't inflation-adjusted push people into higher brackets unfairly, and it's harder to know if a price today is a "good deal" compared to last year. These costs are small at 2% inflation but mount fast at higher rates.

Costs of UNexpected inflation

  • Redistributes wealth from creditors to debtors. If you lent €100 expecting 2% inflation but got 8%, the borrower paid you back in cheaper money — you lost.
In plain English. Surprise inflation is much worse than expected inflation. People sign contracts assuming a certain level of prices — wages, mortgages, business deals. When inflation jumps unexpectedly, anyone owed fixed amounts in money (savers, retirees on fixed pensions, lenders) loses real value, while anyone owing fixed amounts (debtors, mortgage holders) wins. This arbitrary wealth redistribution hurts trust in long-term contracts.

Hyperinflation

When monthly inflation exceeds 50%. Always caused by extreme money printing, usually to finance government deficits. Example: Zimbabwe 2008, Weimar Germany 1923.

Week 9 — Open economy classical + unemployment Mankiw Ch. 6–7

Open economy in the classical model (Ch 6)

Until now we pretended the country was closed. Now we let goods and capital cross borders.

National accounts identity (open)

Y = C + I + G + NX, where NX = exports − imports
Y = output  |  C = consumption  |  I = investment  |  G = government spending  |  NX = net exports (positive = surplus, negative = deficit)

Rearranging: S − I = NX  (national saving minus domestic investment equals net exports). The trade balance equals the gap between national saving and domestic investment.

Trade balance vs budget balance — common exam trap

These two are DIFFERENT concepts but get conflated in exams:

ConceptFormulaSign convention
Trade balanceNX = X − IMPositive = trade surplus (we export more than we import). Negative = trade deficit.
Budget balanceT − G (= public saving)Positive = budget surplus (government collects more than it spends). Negative = budget deficit.
In plain English. Trade balance is about the country's commercial relationship with the rest of the world (exports vs imports). Budget balance is about the government's books (taxes vs spending). They can be in any combination: surplus/surplus, surplus/deficit, deficit/surplus, deficit/deficit. Don't confuse them. A country can have a trade surplus AND a budget deficit, like Denmark often does.
Past exam appearance: V22_RE PS1 asks "explain whether the government budget and trade are in balance" — separate calculations needed for each. V21 A3 asks to identify trade vs budget deficit before suggesting policy.

Worked example: NX as a linear function of e (exchange rate)

A common exam setup: NX = a − b·ε, where b is the sensitivity of NX to the exchange rate.

Example: NX = 1,000 − 125ε  |  if ε = 4 → NX = 1,000 − 500 = 500. If ε rises to 5 → NX = 1,000 − 625 = 375.
Interpretation of coefficient b. The slope coefficient (here 125) measures how sensitive NX is to exchange rate movements. A bigger b means more sensitive trade — a 1-unit increase in ε reduces NX by b units. Trade-heavy economies (like Germany) have higher b; closed economies (like the US relatively) have lower b.
Why? If we save more than we invest at home, the extra savings flow abroad as net foreign lending — we accumulate IOUs from foreigners (i.e., trade surplus). If S < I, we borrow abroad (trade deficit).

Real vs nominal exchange rate

ConceptDefinitionNotation
Nominal exchange ratePrice of one currency in terms of another (e.g., 1 EUR = 7.45 DKK)e
Real exchange ratePrice of domestic goods relative to foreign goods, in same currencyε = e × P/P*

An appreciation means the domestic currency buys more foreign currency (or our goods become more expensive abroad). It hurts exports and helps imports.

In plain English. Nominal exchange rate is what the airport currency-exchange board shows. Real exchange rate adjusts for the fact that prices differ across countries — a beer might cost €5 here and $4 there, so even though the nominal rate is 1 EUR = 1 USD, real purchasing power differs. The real rate determines whether buying domestic vs foreign goods is actually cheaper.

Net exports as a function of the exchange rate

NX = NX(ε) — downward-sloping. ↑ε → ↓X, ↑M → ↓NX.
ε (epsilon) = real exchange rate  |  ↑ε = real appreciation (our goods more expensive abroad)  |  X = exports  |  M = imports here means imports of goods (NOT money supply)  |  NX = net exports
Heads up — letter overload! The letter "M" means two completely different things in macro: money supply (in QTM and money market) AND imports (in open-economy national accounts). Context tells you which. To avoid confusion, the abbreviation IM is sometimes used for imports.

Small open economy with perfect capital mobility

Key assumption: r = r* (domestic real rate must equal world real rate, or capital floods in/out instantly).

Then: domestic I is set by r*, and S − I = NX determines NX, which then determines ε. Capital flows balance the books.

Unemployment (Ch 7)

Definitions

  • Labour force = employed + unemployed (people looking for work but not employed).
  • Unemployment rate u = unemployed / labour force.
  • Labour force participation rate = labour force / working-age population.

Types of unemployment

TypeWhat causes itWhat can reduce it
FrictionalTime needed for workers and jobs to find each other (job search, moving)Better job-matching websites, info, mobility
StructuralWage above market-clearing — minimum wage, unions, efficiency wages, mismatched skillsSkill retraining, lower entry barriers, weakening of wage rigidities
CyclicalBusiness cycle (AD shocks)Counter-cyclical fiscal/monetary policy
In plain English. Not all unemployment is the same. Frictional = "between jobs" (always exists, can't be zero). Structural = "wrong skills or wrong wages" (mismatch — coal miners can't easily become software engineers). Cyclical = "the economy is in recession" (this is what stimulus targets). Only cyclical responds to policy; frictional and structural are baked into how the labour market works.

The natural rate of unemployment un = frictional + structural. It's the unemployment that exists even when the economy is at potential output.

Wage rigidities — why structural unemployment exists

  1. Minimum wage: set above market wage → some workers excluded.
  2. Unions: negotiate wages above market level for members → unemployed outsiders.
  3. Efficiency wages: firms pay above market to motivate workers, deter quitting → fewer hired.

Okun's law

A 1 percentage point increase in u is associated with about a 2% drop in real GDP relative to potential.
Formal form: ΔY/Y̅ ≈ −β·(u − un) with β ≈ 2
Y̅ = potential / natural output  |  un = natural unemployment rate  |  β = Okun coefficient ≈ 2

Sign: negative relationship between unemployment and output. Employment vs output is positive. Don't confuse the two — this is a classic T/F trap.

Sacrifice ratio + adaptive expectations (T/F exam favourite)

Sacrifice ratio = how much output (cumulatively) must be lost to reduce inflation by 1 percentage point.
Typical estimate: ≈ 5 (i.e. 5% of one year's GDP lost per 1 pp π reduction).

Adaptive expectations: πet = πt−1
Expected inflation today equals actual inflation last period.
Why does disinflation cost output? Under adaptive expectations, people expect today's inflation to repeat tomorrow. To bring π down, the central bank must push u above un (recession). Phillips says π = πe − β(u−un); if u > un, then π < πe and people gradually revise their expectations down. This takes time → cumulative output loss = the sacrifice ratio. Short-run trade-off: yes, reducing π requires higher u (the T/F statement in S25/V23 is TRUE for the SR). Long-run trade-off: none — Phillips curve is vertical at un.

Hysteresis — when un depends on past u (recurring exam topic)

In a more advanced Phillips curve, the natural rate un itself can drift with past unemployment:

un,t = 0.25(ut−1 + ut−2 + ut−3 + ut−4)
natural rate today depends on average unemployment over past 4 years
In plain English. When a recession lasts a long time, unemployed workers lose skills, networks atrophy, and some workers become permanently disengaged from the labour market. This raises the LONG-RUN natural rate un, not just temporary u. So even when the economy "recovers" in terms of demand, full employment becomes harder to achieve — the rate the economy can sustain without inflation is permanently higher. This is called hysteresis.

Implication: a long-ago unemployment shock can permanently raise current inflation pressure. If ut−4 was high, then un,t is higher today; with current u fixed, (u−un) is smaller (or negative), and π is higher than it otherwise would be. Past matters.

Past exam appearance: V19 PS3 Q2 asked precisely this. "Explain how a small change in year t−4 will affect the current inflation rate." Answer: through hysteresis, higher historical u raises today's un, which makes the inflation-unemployment trade-off less favourable today.
Memory trick for Okun: Think of it like a leaky tyre. When unemployment rises, the economy's "tyre pressure" (output) goes down. So it's negative. But when more people are employed, the economy runs better — that's positive. Same fact described from opposite sides.
Phillips curve. The red SR Phillips curve slopes downward — short-run trade-off between unemployment and inflation. The dashed blue LR Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate un — no permanent trade-off. They cross at the natural-rate point (un, π = πe). To push u below un short-run, the economy must accept higher π. Long run, expectations adjust and the economy returns to un.

From V19 PS3 Q3 (5%): "How will you differentiate between frictional and structural unemployment?"
Model answer:
Frictional unemployment arises from the time it takes for workers and jobs to find each other. It includes job search, geographical moves, and consideration of offers. Frictional unemployment is unavoidable in a dynamic economy but can be reduced through better job-matching information and labour mobility.

Week 10 — Solow growth model Mankiw Ch. 7 (repeat), 8, 9

Almost certain to be Problem Set 4 on your exam (≈20%).

The big question: why are some countries rich?

Two countries that look similar today can grow apart over time. Solow's model explains long-run growth using three ingredients: capital accumulation, population growth, and technology.

Setup (per worker form)

To make life simple we divide everything by the number of workers L:

  • y = Y/L = output per worker  (lower-case = per worker)
  • k = K/L = capital per worker
  • Production function: y = f(k), with diminishing returns (each extra unit of k adds less)

Standard exam form: y = k1/2 (square root). Sometimes with a scale factor like y = 3·k1/2.

The fundamental equation (with population growth n and tech growth g)

Δk = sf(k) − (δ + n + g) × k
Δk = change in capital per worker  |  s = saving rate (fraction of y saved)  |  f(k) = production function = √k typically  |  δ (delta) = depreciation rate (e.g. 3%)  |  n = population growth rate  |  g = technology growth rate

In words: capital per worker grows when saving per worker (s × f(k)) exceeds break-even investment (δ + n + g) × k. Break-even investment is what's needed to:

  • Replace depreciated capital (δk)
  • Equip new workers as the population grows (nk)
  • Maintain the capital ratio as technology improves and effective workers multiply (gk)
Plain-English picture. Imagine a treadmill. Saving s·f(k) pushes capital per worker upward. Depreciation, more workers, and tech growth all eat away at it. The economy reaches a "steady state" when the upward push exactly cancels the downward drag — you're walking on the treadmill at the same pace as it moves backward. That's k*.
The Solow diagram (with proper geometry). Production y = f(k) (blue) is concave (diminishing returns). Saving sf(k) (green) is a fraction of f(k). Break-even investment (δ+n+g)k (red) is a straight line. The steady-state k* is exactly where the green saving curve crosses the red break-even line — at that point, saving = break-even, so Δk = 0. The blue dot above shows y* at that k. Consumption per worker c* = y* − sy* is the vertical gap between the blue and green curves at k*.

Steady state — the long-run equilibrium

At steady state, Δk = 0, so sf(k*) = (δ + n + g) × k*. With the square-root production function:

sk*1/2 = (δ + n + g)·k*
Divide both sides by k*1/2:
s = (δ + n + g)·k*1/2
k* = [s / (δ + n + g)]²
k* = steady-state capital per worker (the asterisk * means "at steady state")

Once you have k*:

  • y* = √k*  (steady-state output per worker)
  • Saving per worker = s·y*  (s = saving rate, y* = output per worker)
  • Consumption per worker c* = (1 − s)·y*  (c* = steady-state consumption per worker — this is what you want to maximise!)

What changes the steady state?

Changek*y*Long-run growth rate of y
Higher saving rate s↑↑Unchanged (only level, not growth)
Higher population growth n↓↓Unchanged
Higher depreciation δ↓↓Unchanged
Higher technology growth g↓ (in level form, per effective worker)per worker grows at g↑ (this is the only thing that drives long-run per-capita growth)
The key insight Solow taught us: capital alone can't sustain growth — it hits diminishing returns. Only technology improvements drive sustained growth in living standards.

The Golden Rule

The Golden Rule level of capital is the k that maximises consumption per worker in steady state. The condition:

MPK = δ + n + g (at the Golden Rule)
MPK = marginal product of capital (extra output from one more unit of K)  |  δ = depreciation  |  n = population growth  |  g = technology growth. So at the Golden Rule, the return on extra capital exactly equals what's needed to maintain it.
Golden Rule in plain English. Imagine you can choose any saving rate. If you save very little, your steady-state capital and output are small — not much to consume even though you're consuming a big share of a small pie. If you save a lot, capital is huge but you're consuming a tiny share — also not much. Somewhere in the middle is the saving rate that maximises your slice of consumption per worker. That's the Golden Rule. The mathematical condition for that point is MPK = δ + n + g.

Is the steady state always at the Golden Rule? NO. Steady state depends on the saving rate s. The Golden Rule is a property of preferences. Equality only happens for a specific s = sgold:

  • If s < sgold: under-saving. Raising s would (eventually) raise consumption.
  • If s > sgold: over-saving. Lowering s would raise consumption right away.
Why is the steady state usually NOT at the Golden Rule? Because households (or governments) choose s based on their own preferences, taxation, etc. — not based on solving an optimisation problem to maximise long-run consumption. Most countries empirically save less than the Golden Rule rate, because saving means giving up consumption today for higher consumption far in the future, and people heavily discount that distant gain.

Derivation of the Golden Rule condition (so you can write it out from scratch)

Step 1. Consumption per worker in steady state: c* = (1−s)·y* = y* − s·y*
Step 2. In steady state, s·y* equals break-even investment: s·y* = (δ+n+g)·k*
Step 3. So c* = f(k*) − (δ+n+g)·k*
Step 4. To maximise, take derivative w.r.t. k* and set to zero:
  dc*/dk* = f'(k*) − (δ+n+g) = MPK − (δ+n+g) = 0
Step 5. Golden Rule condition: MPK = δ + n + g

Worked Golden Rule calculation (V21 A4)

Setup: y = 3·k1/2, δ = 0.12, n = g = 0.
Step 1. Find MPK: MPK = dy/dk = (3 × ½)·k−1/2 = 1.5/√k
Step 2. Set MPK = δ + n + g: 1.5/√k = 0.12
Step 3. Solve: √k = 1.5/0.12 = 12.5 → kgold = 156.25
Step 4. Compare to actual steady state. If s = 0.24, k* = (0.72/0.12)² = 36. Since 36 < 156.25, this country is below Golden Rule → under-saving → raising s would eventually raise c*.

Comparative statics. Higher saving rate s: the green saving curve shifts UP (more saving at every k). The intersection with the break-even line moves right and up → new steady state at higher k** with higher y*. Week 11 — Economic fluctuations + IS-LM Mankiw Ch. 9 (repeat), 10, 11

Letters you'll see this week (quick reference):
Y = output (total goods produced)  |  Y̅ = potential / natural output
C = consumption  |  I = investment  |  G = government spending  |  T = taxes
Yd = disposable income = Y − T  |  MPC or b = marginal propensity to consume (0 to 1)
r = real interest rate  |  M = money supply  |  P = price level  |  M/P = real money supply
c = interest sensitivity of money demand (just a coefficient)  |  PE = planned expenditure

Why do we need a different model for the short run?

The classical model from Week 6 assumes prices and wages are perfectly flexible — they adjust instantly so that all markets always clear. That's fine for the long run, but it doesn't match how the real world looks over months and quarters.

In the short run, prices are sticky:

  • Wages are negotiated once a year (or longer with union contracts).
  • Restaurants print menus and don't reprint daily; subscription services lock in prices.
  • Many B2B contracts fix prices for 6, 12, or 24 months.

So when demand suddenly drops (say a recession hits), firms can't just cut prices to clear their inventory immediately. Instead, they cut production. Output falls. People get laid off. That's a business cycle. The IS-LM model is built to explain this short-run behaviour.

The "two-speed economy" metaphor. Imagine driving a car. Long run is like cruising on the highway — the car (the economy) is at its potential speed Y̅. Short run is like braking suddenly — wheels lock, the car skids, behavior depends on physics (mass, friction, momentum), not on the engine's potential. Classical theory describes the cruising state; IS-LM describes the skid. Different models for different time horizons of the same economy.

The IS-LM model — overview (Ch 10–11)

The model uses two equations to determine two unknowns simultaneously: output Y and the real interest rate r. We draw it as two curves in a graph with Y on the horizontal axis and r on the vertical axis. Where they intersect is the short-run equilibrium.

  • IS curve: all (Y, r) combinations where the goods market is in equilibrium (planned expenditure equals output). Slopes down.
  • LM curve: all (Y, r) combinations where the money market is in equilibrium (money supply equals money demand). Slopes up.
  • The point where they cross is the only (Y*, r*) that simultaneously clears both markets.
Why two curves? Think of it like finding where you live AND work. Each curve is a "constraint" — a set of possibilities consistent with one piece of information. The IS curve says "given the goods market, here are the (Y, r) pairs that work." The LM curve says "given the money market, here are the (Y, r) pairs that work." The intersection is the unique point that satisfies both — like the one address that's BOTH in your neighbourhood AND near your office.

Building the IS curve — step by step

Start with the goods-market equilibrium condition: total output Y must equal total planned spending. Total planned spending = C + I + G + NX (in an open economy) or just C + I + G (closed).

Now make investment a function of the interest rate: I = I(r), decreasing in r. Higher r makes borrowing expensive, fewer projects look profitable, firms invest less. So I depends on r.

The goods-market equilibrium becomes:

Y = C(Y − T) + I(r) + G  (closed economy, simplest form)
Y = total output  |  C(Y−T) = consumption as a function of disposable income  |  T = taxes  |  I(r) = investment as a function of the real interest rate (decreasing)  |  G = government spending

Now imagine doing a thought experiment. Pick some r — say r = 5%. Solve the equation for Y. You get one specific Y. Now try r = 3%. Re-solve: I is now higher (since r is lower), so PE is higher, so equilibrium Y is higher. Repeat for many r values. Plot each (Y, r) pair on a graph. The dots form a downward-sloping curve. That curve is the IS.

What does the IS curve REALLY represent — in plain English. Imagine you're a detective collecting clues. The IS curve is the collection of every (Y, r) pair where "the goods market would be at peace" — i.e., what firms produce equals what people plan to buy. Lower interest rates make investment cheap → more spending → higher Y. Higher rates → less spending → lower Y. So all these "peaceful" combinations line up as a downward-sloping curve.
The car analogy. Think of r as the price of gas, and Y as how much you drive. When gas is cheap (low r), you drive a lot (high Y). When gas is expensive (high r), you drive less (low Y). Plotting "gas price vs miles driven" gives a downward curve. The IS curve is the same idea: interest rate vs activity in the goods market.

What shifts the IS curve?

Anything that changes spending at a given r shifts IS:

  • ↑G (government spending up) → IS shifts right (more spending at every r → higher Y).
  • ↓T (taxes down) → ↑disposable income → ↑C → IS shifts right.
  • ↑expected future income → ↑C today (PIH/LCH) → IS shifts right.
  • ↑business optimism ("animal spirits") → ↑I at every r → IS shifts right.
  • Opposite changes shift IS left.

The Keynesian cross (the foundation)

Planned Expenditure (PE) = C + I + G
PE = total amount agents plan to spend  |  C = a + b·(Y−T) where b = MPC ∈ (0,1)
At equilibrium, PE = Y. Solving: Y* = (autonomous spending) / (1 − MPC)
"Autonomous spending" = the parts that don't depend on Y, i.e. a (constant in C) − b·T + I + G  |  MPC = marginal propensity to consume
The Keynesian cross. The dashed grey line is PE = Y (the 45° line). The blue line is planned expenditure PE = a + I + G + MPC·Y. They actually intersect at Y*. Above Y*, planned expenditure exceeds output — firms run down inventories, raising Y. Below Y*, PE < Y — inventories pile up, lowering Y. The economy converges to Y*. If G rises, the blue line shifts straight up parallel by ΔG, moving Y* to the right by ΔG/(1−MPC) — that's the multiplier in action.

Multipliers (memorise these — exam favourites)

MPC = marginal propensity to consume (out of one extra dollar of disposable income, how much is spent on C). Always between 0 and 1.

MultiplierFormulaWith MPC = 0.75What it means
Government spending (ΔY/ΔG)1 / (1 − MPC)4↑G of 100 raises Y by 400

Open-economy multiplier in plain English. When the economy is open, some of every additional dollar of income gets spent on imports — that's a "leakage" out of the domestic spending stream. The marginal propensity to import (m) reduces the multiplier. With MPC = 0.75 and m = 0.10: open multiplier = 1/(1 − 0.75 + 0.10) = 1/0.35 = 2.86. With closed economy: 4. The leakage to imports reduces the domestic kick. This is why small open economies have smaller fiscal multipliers than large closed ones.
Why is the multiplier > 1? When G rises by 100, the government spends 100 → that's 100 of income for someone → they spend 75% of it (MPC = 0.75) → that's 75 of income for someone else → they spend 56.25 → … the rounds add up to 100 × 4 = 400.
Balanced-budget multiplier derivation (= 1 exactly). Suppose the government simultaneously raises G AND T by the same amount ΔX. Effect on Y:
  • From ↑G: ΔY1 = ΔX × 1/(1−MPC)
  • From ↑T: ΔY2 = ΔX × (−MPC/(1−MPC))
  • Total: ΔY = ΔX × [1/(1−MPC) − MPC/(1−MPC)] = ΔX × [(1 − MPC)/(1 − MPC)] = ΔX × 1
So a balanced-budget expansion raises Y by EXACTLY the amount spent — no multiplier amplification, but also no contraction. Why the policy comparison "↑G vs ↑T" matters in PS1: for the SAME budget improvement (ΔT = +X vs ΔG = −X), the G-cut hurts Y more because the G-multiplier is bigger in absolute value than the T-multiplier. So if you want to close a deficit with the LEAST GDP loss, raise T rather than cut G.
Open-economy multiplier derivation. Add imports to the equation: IM = m·Y (m = marginal propensity to import). Then Y = C + I + G + X − m·Y. Substituting C = a + MPC·Yd and rearranging: Y[1 − MPC + m] = a + I + G + X − MPC·T. Multiplier = 1/(1 − MPC + m). With MPC = 0.75 and m = 0.10: 1/(1 − 0.75 + 0.10) = 1/0.35 ≈ 2.86, versus 4 in the closed case. Why smaller? Some of each new round of spending leaks abroad as imports, never coming back as domestic income.

Building the LM curve — step by step

The money market is in equilibrium when the supply of money equals the demand for money. Both in real terms (adjusted for prices):

Real money supply = Real money demand  |  M/P = L(Y, r)
M = nominal money supply (set by central bank)  |  P = price level  |  L() = money demand function  |  Y = income (more income → more transactions → more money demand)  |  r = real interest rate (higher r → bonds more attractive than cash → less money demand)

Typical exam form: real money demand is given as a simple linear equation:

(M/P)d = Y − c·r
(M/P)d = real money demanded  |  Y = income (positive effect — more transactions need more money)  |  c = interest sensitivity coefficient (typically a given number like 80 or 100)  |  r = interest rate (negative effect — higher r → less cash held)

Set demand equal to supply:

M/P = Y − c·r  → solve for r as function of Y:  r = (Y − M/P) / c
M = nominal money supply (given)  |  P = price level (given)  |  M/P = real money supply  |  All other letters as above

Now the thought experiment again. Pick a Y, plug in, get r. Repeat for many Y values. Plot (Y, r) pairs. They form an upward-sloping line. That's the LM curve.

The LM curve in plain English. Each point on LM is a "world" where the money market is at peace — supply equals demand. When Y is low, people don't need much money for shopping. With a fixed money supply, the interest rate doesn't need to be high to discourage hoarding. When Y is high, people need lots of money for transactions. With the same fixed money supply, the interest rate must rise to make bonds attractive enough that people don't hoard cash. So higher Y ↔ higher r, giving an upward curve.
The concert ticket analogy. Imagine the central bank has a fixed number of concert tickets (money supply M/P). Demand depends on the size of the crowd wanting them (income Y) and the alternative ticket prices (interest rate r). If a bigger crowd shows up (Y rises) but tickets are still fixed, prices must rise (r rises) so some people give up and walk away. Plot crowd size vs ticket price — upward sloping. That's LM.

What shifts the LM curve?

  • ↑M (central bank prints money) → shifts LM right/down (at any Y, r can be lower to clear the market).
  • ↑P (price level rises) → real M/P falls → shifts LM left/up.
  • ↓M or ↓P → shifts LM in the opposite direction.

Solving IS = LM — the algebra

This is the workhorse of every Problem Set 1. The recipe is mechanical once you understand it:

  1. Write IS in the form Y = f(r). Example: Y = 3,350 − 600r.
  2. Write LM in the form Y = g(r). Example: Y = 1,310 + 80r.
  3. Set them equal (because at equilibrium, the same Y must satisfy both): 3,350 − 600r = 1,310 + 80r.
  4. Solve for r*: collect r terms on one side. 3,350 − 1,310 = 600r + 80r → 2,040 = 680r → r* = 3 (i.e. 3%).
  5. Substitute r* back into either equation to find Y*: Y* = 1,310 + 80(3) = 1,550.
  6. Sanity check: plug r* and Y* into BOTH equations. They should both hold. If they don't, you made an arithmetic mistake.
Why does this work? Setting IS = LM means "find the (Y, r) where both markets are simultaneously at peace." Equivalent to solving two equations in two unknowns. There's typically one unique answer. Geometrically, you're finding where two curves cross.

Under monetary expansion, you instead add a wider pipe to the existing system — pressure drops, more flow gets through everywhere.

The transmission mechanism — how monetary policy reaches the real economy

When the central bank changes M, here's the chain of effects:

  1. ↑M → real money supply rises (assuming P sticky in short run)
  2. At given Y, the equilibrium r in the money market falls (LM shifts right)
  3. Lower r reduces the cost of borrowing for firms
  4. More projects become profitable (MPK > r + δ for more projects) → investment I rises
  5. Higher I means more planned expenditure
  6. Higher PE raises Y (with multiplier effect: ΔY = ΔI / (1−MPC))
  7. Higher Y raises money demand a bit, which dampens the original r drop, but doesn't fully offset it

Final result: Y up, r down. This is exactly what the LM-shift-right picture shows.

Why isn't fiscal policy "free"? The "crowding out" answer matters because policymakers love to claim ↑G has multiplier-4 effects on Y. In a pure Keynesian cross (no r), yes. In IS-LM (with r adjustment), the multiplier shrinks because r rises and squeezes private investment. The empirically observed fiscal multiplier in normal times is closer to 1.0–1.5, not 4.0. (Exception: when r is stuck near zero — the "liquidity trap" — fiscal policy regains its full multiplier.)

Applying IS-LM (Ch 12)

Once you have the basic IS-LM toolkit, you can analyze many real-world events: recessions, the Great Depression, the 2008 crisis, COVID, etc. The model gives you a structured way to think through "what shifts what" and "what happens to Y and r as a result."

Deriving aggregate demand (AD) from IS-LM

The AD curve links the price level P to total output demanded Y. Here's how it falls out of IS-LM:

  1. Suppose P rises (and nothing else changes).
  2. Real money supply M/P falls (same nominal M, higher P).
  3. With less real money, LM shifts left.
  4. The new equilibrium has higher r and lower Y.

So higher P → lower Y. Plot Y on the x-axis and P on the y-axis: the curve slopes down. That's AD. It's not just a "demand curve" in the micro sense — it's the locus of (P, Y) where the IS-LM system would be in equilibrium for each P.

The chain of effects compactly: ↑P → ↓(M/P) → LM shifts left → ↑r → ↓I → ↓Y. That's why AD slopes down.
Each letter: P = price level  |  M = money supply (nominal)  |  M/P = real money supply  |  r = real interest rate  |  I = investment  |  Y = output
Anything that shifts IS or LM at a given P also shifts AD by exactly the same amount.

Mundell-Fleming model (Ch 13) — open economy IS-LM

Now we add international trade and capital flows. The key new assumption is perfect capital mobility: investors can move money across borders instantly with zero cost. As a result:

r = r*
r = domestic real interest rate  |  r* = world real interest rate (taken as given for a "small" country like Denmark)

If r > r*, capital floods in (everyone wants the higher domestic return), forcing r back down. If r < r*, capital floods out. So in equilibrium, r = r* always.

Perfect capital mobility — what it means concretely. Imagine an investor with €1 million. If Danish bonds pay 4% and German bonds pay 5%, the investor instantly moves their money to Germany. Many investors do this simultaneously. Demand for German bonds rises, pushing German yields DOWN. Supply of Danish bonds (people selling) rises, pushing Danish yields UP. This continues until rates equalize. Under "perfect" mobility, this happens in microseconds — there's effectively no delay. In practice, capital controls, taxes, or risk premiums can break this assumption.
Past exam appearance: V20_RE PS1c asked specifically "what is meant by perfect capital mobility?" Answer: it's the assumption that investors can move capital across borders instantly and at no cost, forcing r = r* in a small open economy. The country is a price-taker in the world capital market.
What does "small open economy" mean? Two things. First, the country is "small" — its actions don't affect the world rate r*. Denmark is "small" relative to the global economy in this sense. Second, capital is freely mobile — money can move across borders without restriction. Both assumptions together give us r = r*.
e vs ε — read your exam carefully. Different exams use different symbols for the exchange rate.
  • e = NOMINAL exchange rate (units of foreign currency per unit of home currency, e.g. EUR/DKK). ↑e = home currency stronger = appreciation.
  • ε (epsilon) = REAL exchange rate = e · (P/P*) = how many units of foreign goods you can get per unit of home goods. ↑ε = home goods more expensive abroad in real terms.
In the SHORT run with P and P* fixed (the Mundell-Fleming horizon), e and ε move one-for-one — they are effectively the same variable, just renamed. So a question that writes "NX = 1,000 − 125ε" (V19 PS2) and a question that writes "appreciation reduces NX" (S25 PS3) are using the same mechanism. Use whichever symbol the problem uses, and treat ↑e or ↑ε as "appreciation."

How to think about the four cases without memorising. Two questions determine everything:
Q1: Can r change? No — r = r* always under perfect capital mobility. So any policy that tries to change r is fighting a losing battle: capital flows undo it.
Q2: Can e change? Under floating, YES (market sets it). Under fixed, NO (central bank holds it).
Then for each policy, ask "does it work by changing r or by changing e?" Monetary policy works by changing r (which can't happen) OR by changing M (which works if e can adjust). Fiscal policy works by shifting demand (works if e can NOT adjust to offset it).
That's the logic behind "Floating gives monetary, fixed gives fiscal."

Case 1 walkthrough: Floating + fiscal expansion (the trickiest)

Setup: Germany (floating EUR/USD) raises G. What happens?

  1. ↑G shifts IS* to the right. At the original Y, there's now upward pressure on r.
  2. But r must equal r* (perfect capital mobility). So foreign investors, seeing higher returns, rush in. They need EUR to buy German bonds.
  3. Demand for EUR rises → EUR appreciates (e ↑).
  4. Appreciation makes German exports expensive abroad and imports cheap. NX falls.
  5. The fall in NX exactly offsets the rise in G. Y is unchanged.

Result: Y same, e appreciates. The fiscal expansion didn't increase domestic output — it just shifted demand from German exports to government spending. Pure "expenditure-switching."

Case 2 walkthrough: Floating + monetary expansion

Setup: Germany's central bank prints more money.

  1. ↑M shifts LM* to the right. r-pressure is downward.
  2. But r = r* still. So capital flows OUT (lower domestic returns, investors leave).
  3. Investors sell EUR to buy foreign assets → EUR depreciates (e ↓).
  4. Cheaper EUR makes German exports cheap abroad, imports expensive. NX rises.
  5. Higher NX raises Y.

Result: Y up, e depreciates. The boost to Y comes through trade, not through interest rates.

Case 3 walkthrough: Fixed + fiscal expansion

Setup: Denmark (DKK pegged to EUR) raises G.

  1. ↑G shifts IS* right. Pressure on r and e.
  2. To defend the peg (prevent DKK appreciation), the central bank sells DKK and buys EUR reserves.
  3. Selling DKK increases the money supply (Denmark's M endogenously rises).
  4. ↑M shifts LM* right alongside IS*.
  5. The new equilibrium has higher Y but the same e.

Result: Y up, e fixed. Fiscal gets its full multiplier effect because the CB accommodates by expanding M.

Case 4 walkthrough: Fixed + monetary expansion (impossible)

Setup: Denmark tries to print money.

  1. ↑M would shift LM* right → DKK would depreciate.
  2. But the peg requires constant e. So the CB must buy back DKK by selling reserves.
  3. Buying back DKK reduces M back to its original level.
  4. LM* shifts back to where it was. Nothing changes.

Result: Y unchanged, e unchanged. Monetary policy is mechanically impossible — the CB has surrendered control of M to maintain the peg.

Real-world example: Denmark and the euro. Denmark pegs DKK to EUR (since 1999). This means Denmark's central bank can't really do independent monetary policy — Nationalbanken essentially follows the ECB's rate decisions. Why does Denmark do this? Stability and trade with the EU. But the cost is losing monetary flexibility. In return, fiscal policy is potent (which Denmark has historically used actively). Germany within the eurozone is in the same boat vs other eurozone members but FLOATS against the US dollar — different relationships in different directions.
Memory hook: "Floating gives monetary, fixed gives fiscal." This one sentence is the heart of Mundell-Fleming. Memorize it.
Why is fiscal ineffective under floating? — the intuition that always confuses students.
Imagine the government boosts spending by 100. Normally that would raise Y. But here's what happens in a small open economy with floating rates:
1. Higher Y means higher money demand → upward pressure on r.
2. But r is supposed to equal r* (world rate). So foreign capital floods IN to capture the higher r.
3. Foreign investors must buy the domestic currency to invest here → currency APPRECIATES.
4. Currency appreciation makes our exports expensive abroad, our imports cheap → NET EXPORTS FALL.
5. The fall in NX exactly cancels the original boost from G.
Net result: Y unchanged, e appreciates. The fiscal stimulus was completely "leaked out" via the exchange rate channel.

Under FIXED rates, step 3 is blocked: the central bank prevents the appreciation by selling domestic currency, which expands M, shifts LM* right, and Y rises by the full amount. That's why fiscal is effective under fixed.

Trade policy (tariffs, quotas)

A tariff makes imports more expensive → ↓M (imports) → ↑NX (at any e) → IS* shifts right.

  • Under floating: e appreciates, Y unchanged (LM* still vertical). NX is the same in equilibrium (the appreciation cancels the tariff's effect on net exports).
  • Under fixed: Y rises (CB intervenes to hold e, M expands).

Aggregate Supply (Ch 14)

So far we've drawn AD without thinking carefully about supply. Now we add it.

Three models of short-run AS — same general result

Mankiw gives three theories (sticky-wage, sticky-price, imperfect-information). All boil down to the same thing:

Y = Y̅ + α(P − Pe) — output rises above potential when P > expected P
Y = actual output  |  Y̅ ("Y-bar") = potential or natural output  |  α (alpha) = sensitivity parameter (how much output responds to price surprises)  |  P = actual price level  |  Pe = expected price level
In plain English. Why does SRAS slope upward? Because firms can be tricked by surprises. If prices come in HIGHER than firms expected (P > Pe), they think "great, more profit!" and ramp up production above the long-run level. If prices come in LOWER (P < Pe), they cut production. So output deviates from potential whenever there are price surprises. Long run, expectations catch up and Y returns to Y̅ — that's why LRAS is vertical.

Demand shocks (positive AND negative — recurring exam topic)

Positive demand shock (AD shifts right)

Triggers: ↑G, ↓T, ↑M, sudden ↑consumer confidence, ↑NX from world demand.

↑AD → SR: P↑, Y↑ above Y̅. Over time: Pe rises → SRAS shifts up → returns to Y̅ at higher P. Long run: same Y̅, higher P.
In plain English. A demand boost initially raises BOTH output and prices because firms are caught by surprise. But once they update their expectations, they start charging higher prices for the same output — so over time, the extra output disappears and only the higher price level remains. Demand shocks are "neutral in the long run."
Negative demand shock (AD shifts left)

Triggers: financial crisis, COVID-style demand collapse, ↑pessimism, ↓G, ↑T, ↓M, world recession reducing exports.

↓AD → SR: P↓, Y↓ below Y̅. Over time: Pe falls → SRAS shifts down → returns to Y̅ at lower P. Long run: same Y̅, lower P.
In plain English. A demand drop initially lowers both output and prices. Workers and firms are caught wrong-footed, so production cuts and layoffs happen before prices adjust. Over time, wages and expected prices fall, restoring competitiveness and bringing Y back to potential — but at a lower P level. The painful adjustment period (recession with unemployment) is why policymakers often intervene with stimulus.

Supply shocks (oil shock, COVID, war)

An adverse supply shock shifts SRAS up/left.

  • SR: Y↓, P↑ — both bad. This is stagflation.
  • LR: wages and Pe adjust → SRAS shifts back. Y returns to Y̅, but P stays elevated.
  • Policy options: accommodate (↑M shifts AD right, restores Y but worsens inflation) or wait (slow recovery, but P returns).

A positive (favourable) supply shock shifts SRAS DOWN/right — the mirror image.

  • SR: Y↑, P↓ — the "best of both worlds." Example: oil prices fall sharply, productivity boom, deregulation that lowers input costs.
  • LR: SRAS returns to original (or LRAS itself shifts right if it's a permanent productivity gain). Y returns to a higher Y̅, P stays lower.
  • Policy response: usually no intervention needed — it's a win-win. Possibly slight monetary tightening if the positive supply shock would otherwise overstimulate demand.

The Kuznets puzzle (and how PIH/LCH solve it)

Empirical fact: APC (average propensity to consume = C/Y) is roughly constant in long-run time series, but it FALLS as income rises in cross-section. Keynes alone can't explain this — his theory predicts APC always falls with income.

PIH/LCH solve it: in cross-section, higher-income households have a larger share of transitory income (lucky year, bonus), so APC of permanent income looks lower. In time series, growth is mostly permanent, so APC stays constant.

Letter reminders for the three consumption theories:
Keynes: C = a + b·Yd. C = consumption, a = autonomous (constant), b = MPC, Yd = disposable income = Y − T (here T = taxes).
Friedman: C = α·YP. α (alpha) = constant fraction of permanent income spent  |  YP = permanent income.
Modigliani LCH: C = (W + R·Y)/T. W = wealth (already accumulated)  |  R = remaining working years  |  Y = annual income  |  T (HERE) = total remaining years of life (NOT taxes!).

Implications for policy (e.g., tax rebates)

  • Keynes: a one-time tax rebate raises C immediately by MPC × rebate.
  • PIH/LCH: a one-time rebate is mostly transitory → most of it is saved, very small effect on C.

Empirically, evidence is mixed but tends to favour PIH for predictable, large changes.

Worked LCH numerical example

Setup: A 35-year-old has W = 100,000 DKK in wealth, expects to work R = 30 more years earning Y = 50,000 DKK/year, and expects to live T = 50 more years total.
Step 1. Lifetime resources = W + R·Y = 100,000 + 30 × 50,000 = 100,000 + 1,500,000 = 1,600,000
Step 2. Smooth consumption over T years: C = 1,600,000 / 50 = 32,000 DKK/year
Step 3. Saving while working = Y − C = 50,000 − 32,000 = 18,000 DKK/year
Step 4. In retirement, the person dissaves: spending 32,000 with no labour income — funded by the wealth + saving accumulated during working years.
Plain English: LCH says you don't consume "what you earn this year" — you consume "your share of total lifetime resources spread evenly across remaining years." That's why retirees don't suddenly halve their consumption when income drops to zero.

More on Consumption (Ch 16 finished)

The three classical theories (Keynes, PIH, LCH) capture the foundations, but modern research adds several refinements that explain real-world consumption behaviour:

1. Rational expectations

People use all available information about future income — not just past trends. This means if a policy is announced today (e.g., a tax cut next year), forward-looking households start adjusting today. Implication for policy: "permanent" tax cuts work better than temporary ones, because consumers see through the temporariness.

2. Borrowing constraints

PIH assumes you can borrow against future income. But many households (especially low-income or young) can't. They're "constrained" — current cash flow limits spending. This makes Keynes's "current income matters most" assumption fit better for them. Implication: tax rebates to liquidity-constrained households boost C more than rebates to rich households (who'd save them).

3. Habit formation

People get used to consumption levels. Cutting C is psychologically harder than people expect, so C is smoother than pure PIH/LCH predict. Once you're used to a certain standard of living, it's painful to step down.

4. Behavioural factors (Thaler, Kahneman)

  • Mental accounting: people treat "regular income" differently from "windfall income." A $1000 raise might be spent on essentials; a $1000 tax refund on something fun.
  • Self-control problems: people save less than they say they want to. Auto-enrolment in pensions exploits this.
  • Loss aversion: losses hurt about 2x as much as equivalent gains feel good. Affects risk-taking and saving decisions.

The Theory of Investment (Ch 17)

Investment is the most volatile component of GDP — it can swing dramatically in recessions. Understanding what drives I is key to understanding business cycles.

Three types of investment

  1. Business fixed investment (largest): firms buying machines, factories, equipment, software. Driven by expected profitability.
  2. Residential investment: new housing construction + major renovations. Sensitive to mortgage rates.
  3. Inventory investment (smallest but most volatile): changes in stocks of unsold goods. Tells you about firms' near-term expectations.
In plain English. "Investment" in macro doesn't mean stocks and bonds — it means PHYSICAL spending on capital. A factory built, a house constructed, a warehouse with extra inventory. Investment matters because it's both a component of current Y (demand for new capital) and the source of future Y̅ (the new capital produces output for years). It's also the most volatile component — it swings dramatically in business cycles.

What determines business fixed investment?

Firms compare the cost of capital with the benefits of an extra unit of capital. The classic decision rule:

Cost of capital = r + δ
r = real interest rate (cost of borrowing)  |  δ (delta) = depreciation rate (how much capital wears out per period)

Benefit = MPK
MPK = marginal product of capital — the extra output one more unit of K produces (= α·Y/K for Cobb-Douglas)

Decision rule: Invest if MPK > r + δ. Don't invest (let K shrink via δ) if MPK < r + δ.
Plain-English version. A firm thinks: "If I buy this machine for €100,000 at 5% interest with 10% depreciation, it costs me 15% of its value each year (€15,000). If the machine produces more than €15,000/year of extra output, it's worth buying. Otherwise, skip it." Higher interest rates raise the threshold, making fewer projects profitable. That's why I depends inversely on r.

Result: the investment function I = I(r) is downward-sloping. This is exactly what we used in IS-LM.

What shifts the investment function?

  • ↑Technology (raises MPK at every K) → more I at every r → I curve shifts right
  • ↑Business confidence ("animal spirits") → optimism about future MPK → more I
  • ↑Tax credits for investment → effectively lowers the cost of capital → more I
  • ↑Uncertainty → firms delay irreversible investments → less I (the "option to wait" has value)

Tobin's q theory of investment

q = (market value of firm) / (replacement cost of firm's capital)
q (Tobin's q) = a ratio  |  market value = what investors think the firm is worth (stock market price × shares outstanding)  |  replacement cost = what it would cost to rebuild the firm's capital from scratch today

Decision rule:

  • If q > 1: the market values the firm's capital MORE than it costs to build new capital. Build more capital → invest.
  • If q < 1: the market values the firm LESS than the cost of rebuilding it. Don't invest, even let capital depreciate.

Tobin's q is mathematically related to MPK/cost-of-capital, but framed through the stock market. It explains why investment surges when stock prices boom and collapses when they crash.

Tobin's q in plain English. Imagine you're thinking about opening a coffee shop. You can: (a) buy an existing coffee shop chain on the stock market, or (b) build one from scratch. If existing chains are trading for €100k each (market value) and it costs €150k to build one (replacement cost), q = 0.67. You should BUY the existing one. Nobody builds new coffee shops in this world → I falls. If q = 1.5 (existing chains worth €150k, costs €100k to build), you BUILD → I rises. q-theory says investment tracks q.

Residential investment & inventories — brief

Residential investment is very sensitive to mortgage rates (a form of r). When central banks cut rates, housing booms. The 2007 US housing crash was triggered partly by the Fed raising rates in 2004–06.

Inventory investment is firms' planned vs unplanned changes in stocks. When demand suddenly drops, firms initially absorb it by letting inventories pile up (unplanned positive inventory investment). Then they cut production to bring inventories back down (planned negative inventory investment). This is why inventories are the leading indicator of recessions.

Financial Crises (Ch 22 — newer editions)

What is a financial crisis?

A sharp disruption in financial intermediation — banks and credit markets stop functioning normally. People lose confidence in financial institutions; lending freezes; asset prices collapse. Historical examples: Great Depression (1929), Japan's lost decade (1990), Asian crisis (1997), Global Financial Crisis (2007–08), Euro debt crisis (2010–12).

In plain English. A financial crisis is when the "plumbing" of the financial system breaks. Banks aren't lending. Bonds aren't being sold. People are pulling cash from banks (bank runs). Asset prices are crashing. Suddenly even profitable businesses can't get loans. The credit freeze is much worse than the original asset crash because credit is what keeps the real economy moving — without it, even healthy companies can fail.

How they unfold (typical pattern, in 5 stages)

  1. Asset price boom. Some asset class (housing, dot-com stocks, crypto) starts rising fast. Easy credit fuels it. Everyone seems to be getting rich.
  2. Boom turns to bust. Prices stop rising, leveraged investors panic-sell. Asset prices crash.
  3. Bank balance sheets damaged. Banks holding those assets, or who lent to investors using those assets as collateral, lose money. Some banks become insolvent or near-insolvent.
  4. Credit crunch. Surviving banks tighten lending standards drastically — even creditworthy firms can't borrow. Investment collapses, consumption falls, recession deepens.
  5. Bank runs / panics. Depositors fearing for their savings withdraw cash. This can sink even healthy banks. Confidence collapses; the whole financial system threatens to seize up.
Why are crises so destructive? Because banks aren't just "places to store money" — they're the plumbing of the economy. They turn savings into investment (Y = C + I + G). When they stop working, I collapses, and Y collapses with it. Even a small banking crisis can cause a big recession because everything in the modern economy depends on credit.

Investment under uncertainty (Ch 17)

Beyond the basic I(r) story, several real-world factors drive investment:

1. Expectations & "animal spirits"

Keynes's famous phrase. Investment decisions involve guessing about the future — future demand, future technology, future competition. These guesses depend on confidence, mood, narrative. When the news cycle turns optimistic, firms invest. When pessimism spreads, they hold back even if MPK still exceeds r + δ.

Implication: Investment is more volatile than the simple I = a − br model predicts. Confidence shocks shift the entire I curve.

2. Tax policy

Governments use tax tools to influence investment:

  • Investment tax credits: a percentage of the investment cost is rebated. Effectively lowers the price of capital.
  • Accelerated depreciation: firms can write off capital costs faster for tax purposes → higher after-tax return.
  • Corporate tax rates: lower corporate tax → higher after-tax profits → more incentive to invest.

3. Credit constraints

The standard model assumes firms can borrow at the market rate r. But in practice:

  • Small / new firms can't access capital markets — they rely on bank loans at higher effective rates.
  • During credit crunches (post-crisis), even profitable projects get rejected.
  • Firms with poor collateral pay higher rates than firms with good collateral.

So the "real" r in the I = a − br equation varies across firms. When credit conditions tighten, the effective r for many firms rises, and I falls — even if the central bank's policy rate hasn't moved.

4. Irreversibility & option value

Most investment is irreversible — once you've built a factory, you can't easily un-build it. This makes WAITING valuable when there's uncertainty: "let me see how things shake out before committing." Higher uncertainty → more waiting → less investment, even at favourable interest rates.

Money Supply, Money Demand, Banking (Ch 18)

What is money? Three functions

  1. Medium of exchange: you can use it to buy stuff. (Cash, bank deposits qualify; gold not really anymore.)
  2. Store of value: it holds value over time. (Inflation erodes this for cash.)
  3. Unit of account: we measure prices in it (Euros, Dollars).
In plain English. Anything that fulfils all three roles is "money." Cash and bank deposits do — they're spendable, they hold value (modulo inflation), and we quote prices in them. Gold once did but no longer — you can't easily spend gold at the supermarket. The three functions are how we define money in macro, regardless of physical form.

Measures of money supply

  • Monetary base (B or M0): currency in circulation + bank reserves at the central bank. Directly controlled by the CB. B = monetary base  |  M0 = the same thing, alternative notation
  • M1: currency + checking deposits (immediately spendable).
  • M2: M1 + savings deposits, small time deposits (slightly less liquid but easily converted).
  • M3: M2 + larger time deposits and money market funds (even less liquid).
Quick letter note. When we wrote "M" in IS-LM (M/P real money supply), we meant roughly M1 or M2 — the "spendable" money in the economy. Don't confuse M (money) with M (imports) from open-economy chapters!

How banks "create" money — the fractional reserve system

The central bank creates the monetary base B. Commercial banks then multiply this through fractional-reserve lending — they hold only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend out the rest.

Step-by-step example. Reserve ratio rr = 10%.

Round 1. You deposit 1,000 DKK at Bank A. Bank A holds 100 (= 10% reserve) and lends out 900.
After Round 1: deposits = 1,000, reserves = 100, loans = 900. Money supply (counting deposits) = 1,000.

Round 2. The 900 borrower spends it. The recipient deposits 900 at Bank B. Bank B holds 90 reserve, lends 810.
After Round 2: total deposits = 1,000 + 900 = 1,900. Total reserves = 100 + 90 = 190. Total loans = 900 + 810 = 1,710.

Round 3. The 810 gets deposited at Bank C → 81 reserve, 729 lent.
Total deposits now: 1,000 + 900 + 810 = 2,710.

The infinite geometric series: 1,000 × (1 + 0.9 + 0.9² + 0.9³ + ...) = 1,000 × (1 / 0.1) = 10,000 DKK of total deposits created from the original 1,000 of monetary base.
Why is this called "money creation"? Banks didn't print currency. But they expanded the broadly-defined money supply (M1, M2). When you deposit 1,000, you still have 1,000 of "money" (now in your bank account). The person who got your 900 loan ALSO has 900 of money (which they then spent). The 900 didn't come out of nowhere — it came from the bank "creating" a new deposit by writing a loan. Modern money is mostly bank deposits, not central bank cash. So banks really do create most of the money supply.

The money multiplier (simple version)

m = 1 / rr, where rr = reserve ratio
m = money multiplier (NOT the same as MPC or marginal propensity to import — letters get reused!)  |  rr = fraction of deposits banks must hold as reserves (e.g. 0.10 = 10%)  |  B = monetary base (cash + bank reserves at central bank)  |  M = total money supply

Money supply M = m × monetary base B. So if B = 1,000 and rr = 0.10, M = 10,000.

Money demand

People hold money for transactions and as a store of value. Demand depends on Y (more activity → more money needed) and i (higher i → more attractive to hold bonds, less attractive to hold cash).

L(Y, i) — money demand. ↑Y → ↑demand. ↑i → ↓demand.
L() is just the symbol for "the money demand function." Y = real income, i = nominal interest rate. So money demand depends on income (positively — more transactions need more money) and the nominal rate (negatively — higher i makes bonds more attractive than cash).
The money market. Money supply M/P is fixed (vertical blue line). Money demand slopes down (higher i means people prefer bonds over cash). The black dot sits exactly where the supply line crosses the demand curve — that's the equilibrium interest rate i*. If Y rises, the demand curve shifts right and i must rise — this is exactly why the LM curve slopes up.
Why the LM curve slopes up — the simplest version. Think of the money market like a parking lot. Money supply is the number of parking spaces, fixed. Money demand is how many cars want to park. When the economy is busy (high Y), more cars need parking. With fixed spaces, the parking fee (i) must rise to keep things balanced. So higher Y means higher i. Plot that relationship and it slopes up. That's LM.

Government Debt (Ch 19)

The basic definitions

  • Budget deficit (in year t) = Gt − Tt. The flow — how much more the government spent than collected this year.
  • Government debt (at time t) = Dt = sum of all past deficits (minus any surpluses). The stock — total accumulated borrowings.
  • Debt-to-GDP ratio = D / nominal Y. The most relevant sustainability indicator — debt scaled to the economy's size.
In plain English. Deficit is what happens in ONE year (this year's overspend). Debt is the running TOTAL across all past years. Like the difference between "this month's credit card bill" (deficit) vs. "total credit card balance" (debt). The relevant question for a government's solvency isn't the absolute size of debt but how big it is RELATIVE to the economy — that's the debt-to-GDP ratio.
Why debt-to-GDP, not absolute debt? A country with €10 trillion of debt is in big trouble if its GDP is €1 trillion (1000% ratio), but fine if GDP is €100 trillion (10% ratio). The economy's size determines how easily debt can be serviced (taxed away). Just like for an individual, what matters is debt relative to income.

How debt evolves over time

ΔD = (G − T) + r·D  (new debt added per year = primary deficit + interest on existing debt)
D = stock of government debt  |  G − T = primary deficit (spending minus tax revenue, excluding interest)  |  r = real interest rate paid on debt

Debt-to-GDP dynamics: Δ(D/Y) ≈ (G−T)/Y + (r − g)·(D/Y)
D/Y = debt-to-GDP ratio  |  g = real GDP growth rate  |  r − g = the "spread" that determines if debt explodes or stabilises

This formula reveals the famous condition for sustainability:

When is debt sustainable?

If the real interest rate r is below the growth rate g, debt-to-GDP can be stable (or even fall) even with persistent primary deficits. This is the famous "r < g" condition:

  • If r < g: the economy grows faster than the debt does (via interest). Even with deficits, D/Y stays manageable. Denmark, Germany have benefited from this.
  • If r > g: the economy can't outrun the interest bill. Debt explodes without primary surpluses to offset.
Why has r < g been the norm recently? From around 2010 to 2022, real interest rates were unusually low (sometimes negative!) while economies still grew. This made government debt "free" in a sense — countries could borrow and grow out of it. Post-2022, with higher inflation and tightening, r > g is reappearing in some countries, raising concerns about debt sustainability.

Two views on whether debt matters

Traditional / Keynesian view

Higher G with no offsetting T cut means:

  1. Public saving falls (T − G is more negative)
  2. National saving (private + public) falls
  3. Loanable funds supply shrinks → r rises
  4. Higher r → less private investment I
  5. Long-run capital stock is lower → lower Y̅ for future generations

Conclusion: Deficits crowd out investment. Future generations are worse off.

In plain English. The traditional view: when the government borrows more, there's less pool of savings left for private firms to borrow from. So interest rates rise, and businesses build fewer factories. The country ends up with less capital, lower potential output, lower wages. Today's deficit is essentially "borrowing from the future" — your kids pay through having a smaller, less productive economy.
Ricardian equivalence (Robert Barro, 1974)

Forward-looking, rational households recognise that today's deficit means future taxes (the government must repay the debt eventually). They save more today to pay those future taxes. Private saving rises by exactly the amount public saving fell. National saving is unchanged. Deficits don't crowd out investment.

If true, this means debt-financed tax cuts have no effect on consumption or output. Households just save the rebate.

In plain English. Barro's twist: imagine the government gives you a €500 tax cut, financed by borrowing. A smart household thinks "this isn't free money — the government will have to tax me €500 (plus interest) later to repay the debt." So they save the €500 to pay that future tax. Net effect on consumption: zero. Net effect on national saving: zero. The deficit just shifts who's saving (less government, more households), not the total. Reality: only PARTIALLY true — most people aren't this forward-looking.
Which view is right? Empirically, Ricardian equivalence holds partially but not fully. Reasons it doesn't hold completely:
• People don't live forever — they may not care about future generations who'll pay the taxes.
• People face borrowing constraints — they can't easily save current windfalls.
• Future taxes are uncertain — maybe the next government will cancel the debt or default.
• Many people simply aren't that forward-looking.
Most economists believe deficits raise national saving partially but not fully — somewhere between the two extremes.

Other concerns about debt

  • Interest burden: servicing debt requires taxes, which distort incentives.
  • Vulnerability to crisis: high-debt countries are more vulnerable to confidence shocks (Greek crisis 2010).
  • Crowding out (back to traditional view).
  • Intergenerational fairness: current generation enjoys spending; future generation pays.
  • Inflation risk: if debt becomes unsustainable, the government may "monetise" it (print money to repay) → inflation.

Stabilization Policy (Ch 21)

Should the government try to smooth out business cycles?

This is one of the biggest debates in macroeconomics. Two camps:

ViewRecommendsWhy
Active stabilization (Keynesian)Use fiscal and monetary policy actively to dampen cycles.Recessions are extremely costly (unemployment, lost output, wasted human capital). Markets fail to self-correct quickly. We have the tools — use them.
Passive policy (Classical / monetarist / new classical)Let markets adjust on their own. Use simple rules, not discretion.Active policy is plagued by lags, mistakes, and political distortions. Often makes things worse rather than better. Markets self-correct over time. Just keep policy predictable.

Why active policy is hard — three lags

  1. Recognition lag. Economic data comes with a delay (GDP figures arrive months later, revised again later). You don't know you're in a recession until well after it started.
  2. Implementation lag. Even after recognising the problem, taking action takes time. Fiscal legislation must pass parliament — can take months or years. Monetary decisions are faster (weeks) but still not instant.
  3. Effectiveness lag. Once policy is in place, it takes 6–18 months to fully work through the economy (the transmission mechanism we saw in Week 11).

Total lag from recession start to policy effect can easily be 1–2 years. By the time stimulus hits, the recession may be over — and you're now over-stimulating a recovery, causing inflation. This is Milton Friedman's "long and variable lags" critique of active policy.

The shower analogy. Imagine a shower with very slow water-temperature controls. You turn the hot tap up because the water's cold — but the change takes 30 seconds. By then, the water is now too hot. You turn it down — now too cold again. You oscillate forever instead of finding the right temperature. Active stabilisation policy can be like this if the lags are too long: you keep over-correcting and amplifying cycles instead of dampening them.

Automatic stabilisers — a middle ground

Some policies stabilise without needing active decisions:

  • Progressive income tax: in a boom, tax revenue rises automatically (people earn more); in a recession, it falls. Smooths disposable income.
  • Unemployment insurance: in a recession, more people claim it, automatically supporting consumption.
  • Welfare programs: similar countercyclical effect.

Most economists agree these "built-in" stabilisers are good. The debate is about discretionary (active) policy on top of them.

Rules vs Discretion

Discretion: central bank decides each meeting based on judgement. Pros: flexibility, can respond to unique circumstances. Cons: political pressure, inflation bias, inconsistent.

Rules: the central bank follows a fixed formula. Pros: predictable, removes political influence, anchors expectations. Cons: inflexible, can't handle unique shocks well.

The famous Taylor rule (John Taylor, 1993) prescribes:

i = r* + π + 0.5(π − π*) + 0.5·(Y − Y̅)/Y̅
i = nominal policy interest rate (what the central bank sets)  |  r* = neutral / equilibrium real rate (typically ~2%)  |  π = current inflation  |  π* (pi-star) = the central bank's inflation target (usually 2%)  |  Y = current output  |  Y̅ = potential output  |  (Y − Y̅)/Y̅ = output gap (the % deviation from potential — positive in booms, negative in recessions)
How to read the Taylor rule. It says the central bank should raise i if (a) inflation π is above target π*, or (b) the economy is above potential (Y > Y̅). The 0.5 weights say "weight each gap equally." If π = π* = 2% and Y = Y̅, then i = r* + π = 2% + 2% = 4% (a "neutral" setting).

Worked Taylor rule example

Setup: r* = 2%, π = 4%, π* = 2%, output gap (Y−Y̅)/Y̅ = +1% (booming).
Step 1. Compute inflation gap: π − π* = 4% − 2% = 2 pp
Step 2. Output gap given: 1%
Step 3. Apply: i = r* + π + 0.5(π−π*) + 0.5·(Y−Y̅)/Y̅
Step 4. Substitute: i = 2 + 4 + 0.5(2) + 0.5(1) = 2 + 4 + 1 + 0.5 = 7.5%
Interpretation: the CB should set the policy rate above neutral (4%) because BOTH inflation is above target AND the economy is overheating. The rule "automatically" tightens by 3.5 pp above neutral.

Many central banks effectively follow Taylor-like rules even though they don't formally commit to them.

Time inconsistency (Kydland & Prescott, Nobel 2004)

A surprising argument for rules. Suppose the central bank wants low inflation AND high output (above potential). Here's the game:

  1. CB announces: "We'll keep inflation at 2%."
  2. Workers and firms believe it. They set wages and contracts assuming 2% inflation.
  3. Once wages are set, the CB now has an INCENTIVE to surprise: print more money → inflation rises above 2% → real wages fall → firms hire more → Y rises above Y̅.
  4. But workers aren't fools. They learn the CB will surprise them. So they set wages expecting higher inflation in the first place.
  5. Final outcome: high inflation AND output back at Y̅. The CB got the worst of both worlds.

Solution: the CB must commit to a rule that removes its discretion. This way, workers trust the announcement. Modern central bank independence is precisely about making this commitment credible — by removing inflation policy from politicians who can't credibly commit to low inflation.

Time inconsistency in everyday life. "I'll start dieting tomorrow" — well-intentioned, but tomorrow becomes today and you face the same choice. You eat the cake. The "diet starting tomorrow" plan is time-inconsistent. The solution? Commitment devices: don't buy the cake in the first place. Central bank independence is the macro version of "throw out the cake" — remove the inflation lever from politicians who can't resist using it.

"A higher tax rate reduces disposable income, which by the consumption function lowers C. Lower C reduces aggregate demand, lowering Y (in our IS-LM example, from 1,550 to ~1,497). The policy involves a classic trade-off: short-run pain (lower Y, higher unemployment) for long-run gain (less debt, more I, healthier public finances). Whether it's wise depends on the state of the economy. If the economy is below potential, the contractionary effect deepens the recession — bad timing. If the economy is overheating with high inflation, contractionary fiscal makes sense. So context matters."

The five families of formulas (with every letter explained)

  1. National accounts: Y = C + I + G (+NX); S − I = NX; multiplier = 1/(1−MPC).
    Y = output, C = consumption, I = investment, G = government spending, NX = net exports (X − IM), S = saving, MPC = marginal propensity to consume.
  2. Money: M·V = P·Y; i = r + πe; M/P = L(Y, i).
    M = money supply, V = velocity of money, P = price level, i = nominal interest, r = real interest, πe = expected inflation, L() = money demand function.
  3. Production: Y = A·Kα·L(1−α); MPL = (1−α)Y/L; MPK = αY/K.
    A = technology, K = capital, L = labour, α = capital share, 1−α = labour share, MPL = marginal product of labour, MPK = marginal product of capital.
  4. Solow: Δk = sf(k) − (δ+n+g)k; k* = (s/(δ+n+g))²; golden rule MPK = δ+n+g.
    k = capital per worker, s = saving rate, δ = depreciation rate, n = population growth rate, g = technology growth rate, k* = steady-state k.
  5. Phillips/Okun: π = πe − β(u−un) + ν; ΔY/Y ≈ −β(u−un).
    π = inflation, πe = expected inflation, β = sensitivity coefficient, u = unemployment rate, un = natural rate of unemployment, ν = supply shock.
