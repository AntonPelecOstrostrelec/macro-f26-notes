Week 6 — Intro, GDP, CPI, classical national income Mankiw Ch. 1–4

What is macroeconomics, really?

Microeconomics looks at one market — apples, jeans, software developers. Macroeconomics zooms out to the whole economy at once: total output, total employment, prices in general, interest rates. Instead of asking "what's the price of bread?" macro asks "are prices rising in general (inflation)?"

Three central questions of macro:
1. Why do some countries get rich while others stay poor? (growth)
2. Why does the economy go through booms and busts? (business cycles)
3. What can the government do to keep things stable? (policy)

Models, exogenous, endogenous

An economic model is a simplified picture — usually a few equations and a graph. We feed it some inputs and the model tells us the outputs.

  • Exogenous variables = things determined outside the model. We just take them as given. Examples: government spending G, the money supply M, the world interest rate r*.
  • Endogenous variables = things determined inside the model. The model explains them. Examples: GDP (Y), the interest rate r, the price level P.

Think of a recipe: ingredients are exogenous (you put them in), the cake is endogenous (the recipe produces it).

GDP — Gross Domestic Product (Ch 2)

GDP = the total market value of all final goods and services produced inside a country in a given period (usually a year or quarter).

Three ways to measure GDP — they all give the same number:
1. Production approach: add up the value of everything produced (avoiding double-counting by using only final goods, or summing value-added at each stage).
2. Expenditure approach: GDP = C + I + G + NX  (C = consumption, I = investment, G = government spending, NX = net exports = exports − imports).
3. Income approach: add up all wages, profits, rents, interest paid in the economy.

Nominal vs Real GDP

DefinitionWhy we care
Nominal GDPOutput valued at current pricesMixes up real growth with inflation — misleading for comparing years.
Real GDPOutput valued at base-year (constant) pricesStrips out inflation, so changes reflect actual production changes.
Example. In 2024 the country produced 100 cars at €20,000 each → nominal GDP = 2,000,000. In 2025 the same 100 cars are made but the price rose to €25,000 → nominal GDP = 2,500,000 (looks like 25% growth). Real GDP using 2024 prices = 100 × 20,000 = 2,000,000 in BOTH years — actual production didn't change. The 25% is purely inflation.

GDP Deflator

GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100
Nominal GDP = output at current prices  |  Real GDP = output at constant base-year prices

It's the price index implied by GDP. Uses current quantities as weights — economists call this a Paasche index.

CPI — Consumer Price Index

The CPI measures the cost of a fixed basket of consumer goods, year by year. Statistics Denmark picks a representative basket once and then tracks its cost over time. Uses fixed weights — a Laspeyres index.

CPI = (Cost of basket at current prices / Cost of basket at base prices) × 100
CPI vs Deflator — the trap question: CPI uses fixed weights; the GDP deflator uses current quantities. The exam loves to reverse this.

Inflation

π = (CPIt − CPIt−1) / CPIt−1 × 100
π (Greek letter "pi") = inflation rate  |  CPIt = price index this period  |  CPIt−1 = price index last period

If CPI rises from 105 to 108, inflation = 3/105 ≈ 2.86%.

Other useful aggregates

  • GNP = GDP + net income from abroad. (What citizens earn, regardless of where they work.)
  • NNP = GNP − depreciation. (What's left after replacing worn-out capital.)
  • Disposable income (Yd) = Y − T. What households actually have to spend.

Classical Theory of National Income (Ch 3)

This is the long-run view: prices are flexible, markets clear, everything is determined by real factors.

The production function

Y = F(K, L) — output depends on capital and labour.
Y = total output  |  K = capital stock (machines, factories)  |  L = labour (workers)

Standard assumption: constant returns to scale (CRS). Doubling both K and L doubles Y. The Cobb-Douglas form is most common: Y = A·Kα·L(1−α)  (A = technology, α (alpha) = capital's share of income, 1−α = labour's share).

How factors get paid

In a competitive market, firms hire each factor up to the point where the value of one more unit equals what it costs:

  • Hire one more worker until real wage W/P = MPL (marginal product of labour). MPL = ∂Y/∂L.
  • Rent one more unit of capital until real rent R/P = MPK. MPK = ∂Y/∂K.
Why? Plain-English logic. If hiring an extra worker produces 5 units of output (worth 5×P euros) but you only pay them W euros, you'd hire another. You keep hiring until the marginal worker's output exactly covers their wage — i.e., MPL = W/P. Same logic for capital.

Income shares

For Cobb-Douglas Y = Kα·L(1−α):

  • Labour income = MPL × L = (1−α) × Y. So labour gets share (1−α) of total output.
  • Capital income = MPK × K = α × Y. Capital gets share α.
  • Together they sum to Y exactly (Euler's theorem). No leftover, no shortage.

How total income gets divided up between C, I, G

In the classical model, output Y is fixed by the production function. The interest rate r adjusts so that saving = investment in equilibrium:

Sprivate = Y − T − C,   Spublic = T − G,   Snational = Sprivate + Spublic = Y − C − G
Equilibrium: S = I(r)
S = saving (private + public)  |  T = taxes  |  r = real interest rate  |  I(r) = investment as a function of r (decreasing)
What does this mean in plain English? Think of it like a giant pot of money. Households save some of their income → that's the supply of loanable funds. Firms want to borrow money to invest → that's the demand. The interest rate r is the "price" that makes the pot balance. If saving is high relative to demand, r falls. If borrowing demand is high, r rises.
The loanable funds market. Saving (vertical because Y is fixed in the long run) meets investment demand. The equilibrium real interest rate r* clears the market.

If government spending rises (↑G), national saving falls (because Spub = T − G falls). The S curve shifts left, raising r and reducing I. This is crowding out — even in the classical model, government spending squeezes out private investment.

The Monetary System (Ch 4)

What money is and what it does

Money is anything that's a (1) medium of exchange, (2) store of value, (3) unit of account. Cash, bank deposits, etc.

Money supply (M)

Controlled by the central bank through several tools (open market operations, reserve requirements, the discount rate). For now we just take M as exogenous.

Quantity Theory of Money (QTM)

M × V = P × Y
M = money supply (controlled by central bank)  |  V = velocity (how often each money unit gets used per year)  |  P = price level  |  Y = real output
In growth-rate form: %ΔM + %ΔV = %ΔP + %ΔY
If V is constant: π = %ΔM − %ΔY  (inflation = money growth − real output growth)
Plain-English meaning: If the central bank prints 10% more money but real output only grows 2%, prices rise by 8%. Money beyond what the economy actually produces just bids up prices.

The classical dichotomy and money neutrality

Classical economists separated real variables (Y, K, L, real wages) from nominal variables (P, M, nominal wages). In the long run, changes in M only affect nominal stuff — Y, employment, real wages stay the same. This is called money neutrality.

Fisher Equation

i = r + πe    (nominal interest = real interest + expected inflation)
i = nominal interest rate (what you see on a bank statement)  |  r = real interest rate (purchasing-power return)  |  πe = expected inflation

If you're a lender, you want a real return r. If you also expect inflation of 3%, you'll demand i = r + 3% nominally so the real return is still r. Real interest rate r = i − π.

Exam check (Week 6) — Past exam question + worked solution:

From V19 PS1 (10%): "The Fisher equation states i = r + π. Assume the nominal interest rate is 3% and CPI is 125. The Central Bank's monetary policy raises CPI to 126. How will this affect the real interest rate?"
Step 1 — find inflation: π = (126 − 125)/125 × 100 = 0.8%.
Step 2 — apply Fisher: r = i − π = 3% − 0.8% = 2.2%.
Step 3 — interpret: The real return on saving is only 2.2%. The 3% nominal return is partially eroded by the 0.8% inflation. Inflation-adjusted purchasing power grows at 2.2%, not 3%.
From V20 PS2a (10%): "Cobb-Douglas Y = AKαL(1−α) with α = 0.6, K = 25, L = 50, A = 1. Calculate MPL and MPK."
Step 1 — compute Y: Y = 1 × 250.6 × 500.4 ≈ 6.9 × 4.78 ≈ 33.
Step 2 — MPL = (1−α)·Y/L: MPL = 0.4 × 33/50 = 0.264.
Step 3 — MPK = α·Y/K: MPK = 0.6 × 33/25 = 0.792.
Shortcut: Labour income share is always (1−α) = 40%, capital share is α = 60%, regardless of K, L, A.
Other Week-6 topics that come up: CPI vs GDP deflator weights (definite T/F trap); real vs nominal GDP (recurring T/F); QTM growth-rate equation %ΔP = %ΔM − %ΔY.

Week 8 — Classical theory continued + inflation Mankiw Ch. 3–5

Week 8 deepens what we started in week 6: the classical model of how prices and output are set, plus a chapter on inflation in detail.

Loanable funds market (Ch 3 deep dive)

In classical theory, the interest rate r is set in the market for loanable funds. Saving is the supply, investment is the demand:

  • Savings supply: S = Y − C − G (vertical line if Y is fixed by production).
  • Investment demand: I = I(r), downward-sloping (lower r → cheaper to borrow → more I).

Equilibrium r is where S = I.

Government deficits and saving

Suppose ↑G with T unchanged → public saving (T − G) falls → national saving falls → S curve shifts left → r rises → I falls. This is the classical version of crowding out.

Inflation: causes, effects, costs (Ch 5)

The cause: long-run inflation = money growth (mostly)

In the long run, with V and Y stable, π ≈ %ΔM. So if Denmark's central bank grows the money supply at 8% per year while real output grows 2%, inflation will be about 6%.

The Fisher effect

If money growth rises by 5 percentage points, expected inflation rises by 5 pp, and the nominal interest rate rises by 5 pp too. The real rate is unaffected. This is the Fisher effect.

Costs of expected inflation

  • Shoe-leather costs: people hold less cash to avoid inflation, which means more trips to the ATM (literally wearing out shoe leather).
  • Menu costs: firms have to keep changing prices.
  • Tax distortions: tax codes are usually written in nominal terms, so inflation distorts effective tax rates.
  • Confusion: harder to compare prices across years.

Costs of UNexpected inflation

  • Redistributes wealth from creditors to debtors. If you lent €100 expecting 2% inflation but got 8%, the borrower paid you back in cheaper money — you lost.

Hyperinflation

When monthly inflation exceeds 50%. Always caused by extreme money printing, usually to finance government deficits. Example: Zimbabwe 2008, Weimar Germany 1923.

Exam check (Week 8) — Past exam question + worked solution:

From V20 PS2c (5%): "The Quantity Theory of Money (QTM) implies the price level is proportional to the quantity of money. How is the inflation rate determined according to QTM?"
Model answer: "QTM states M·V = P·Y. Taking growth rates: %ΔM + %ΔV ≈ %ΔP + %ΔY. With velocity V approximately constant in the long run (assumption of QTM): %ΔP ≈ %ΔM − %ΔY. So inflation equals the money growth rate minus the real output growth rate. Numerical example: if M grows at 8% and real Y grows at 2%, inflation will be approximately 6%."
From V19_RE PS1 (concept + numerical): "Suppose money supply grows at 50%/year, real GDP at 3%/year, velocity constant. Find inflation."
Calculation: π ≈ %ΔM − %ΔY = 50% − 3% = 47%. This is hyperinflation territory — confirming that runaway money printing causes runaway inflation, exactly as the classical dichotomy predicts.
Money neutrality T/F (every year): "In the long run, changes in M affect only P, not real Y, real r, or employment" — TRUE. The classical dichotomy.

Week 9 — Open economy classical + unemployment Mankiw Ch. 6–7

Open economy in the classical model (Ch 6)

Until now we pretended the country was closed. Now we let goods and capital cross borders.

National accounts identity (open)

Y = C + I + G + NX, where NX = exports − imports
Y = output  |  C = consumption  |  I = investment  |  G = government spending  |  NX = net exports (positive = surplus, negative = deficit)

Rearranging: S − I = NX  (national saving minus domestic investment equals net exports). The trade balance equals the gap between national saving and domestic investment.

Why? If we save more than we invest at home, the extra savings flow abroad as net foreign lending — we accumulate IOUs from foreigners (i.e., trade surplus). If S < I, we borrow abroad (trade deficit).

Real vs nominal exchange rate

ConceptDefinitionNotation
Nominal exchange ratePrice of one currency in terms of another (e.g., 1 EUR = 7.45 DKK)e
Real exchange ratePrice of domestic goods relative to foreign goods, in same currencyε = e × P/P*

An appreciation means the domestic currency buys more foreign currency (or our goods become more expensive abroad). It hurts exports and helps imports.

Net exports as a function of the exchange rate

NX = NX(ε) — downward-sloping. ↑ε → ↓X, ↑M → ↓NX.
ε (epsilon) = real exchange rate  |  ↑ε = real appreciation (our goods more expensive abroad)  |  X = exports  |  M = imports here means imports of goods (NOT money supply)  |  NX = net exports
Heads up — letter overload! The letter "M" means two completely different things in macro: money supply (in QTM and money market) AND imports (in open-economy national accounts). Context tells you which. To avoid confusion, the abbreviation IM is sometimes used for imports.

Small open economy with perfect capital mobility

Key assumption: r = r* (domestic real rate must equal world real rate, or capital floods in/out instantly).

Then: domestic I is set by r*, and S − I = NX determines NX, which then determines ε. Capital flows balance the books.

Unemployment (Ch 7)

Definitions

  • Labour force = employed + unemployed (people looking for work but not employed).
  • Unemployment rate u = unemployed / labour force.
  • Labour force participation rate = labour force / working-age population.

Types of unemployment

TypeWhat causes itWhat can reduce it
FrictionalTime needed for workers and jobs to find each other (job search, moving)Better job-matching websites, info, mobility
StructuralWage above market-clearing — minimum wage, unions, efficiency wages, mismatched skillsSkill retraining, lower entry barriers, weakening of wage rigidities
CyclicalBusiness cycle (AD shocks)Counter-cyclical fiscal/monetary policy

The natural rate of unemployment un = frictional + structural. It's the unemployment that exists even when the economy is at potential output.

Wage rigidities — why structural unemployment exists

  1. Minimum wage: set above market wage → some workers excluded.
  2. Unions: negotiate wages above market level for members → unemployed outsiders.
  3. Efficiency wages: firms pay above market to motivate workers, deter quitting → fewer hired.

Okun's law

A 1 percentage point increase in u is associated with about a 2% drop in real GDP relative to potential.

Sign: negative relationship between unemployment and output. Employment vs output is positive. Don't confuse the two — this is a classic T/F trap.

Memory trick for Okun: Think of it like a leaky tyre. When unemployment rises, the economy's "tyre pressure" (output) goes down. So it's negative. But when more people are employed, the economy runs better — that's positive. Same fact described from opposite sides.
Phillips curve: short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment, but long run is vertical at the natural rate (no permanent trade-off).
Exam check (Week 9) — Past exam question + worked solution:

From V19 PS3 Q3 (5%): "How will you differentiate between frictional and structural unemployment?"
Model answer:
Frictional unemployment arises from the time it takes for workers and jobs to find each other. It includes job search, geographical moves, and consideration of offers. Frictional unemployment is unavoidable in a dynamic economy but can be reduced through better job-matching information and labour mobility.

Structural unemployment arises when the wage rate is set above the market-clearing level, so the quantity of labour supplied exceeds the quantity demanded. Three classic causes are: minimum wage laws, union bargaining, and efficiency wages (firms voluntarily paying above market). Structural unemployment is reduced by lowering wage rigidities, retraining workers, and matching skills to demand.

Together: Frictional + structural = the natural rate of unemployment un, the rate that exists even at potential output.
From V21 A2 Q2-3 (10%): "Business cycles drive real GDP up and down. How is this related to unemployment via Okun's law? And via the Phillips curve to inflation?"
Okun part: Real GDP and unemployment move opposite. In a boom, Y rises above Y̅, firms hire more, u falls below un. Quantitatively, Okun's law: a 1 pp drop in u corresponds to roughly a 2% rise in Y above potential.
Phillips part: Lower unemployment then triggers wage and price pressure: π = πe − β(u − un) + ν. With u below un, inflation rises above expected. So the booms produce inflation. In recessions, the opposite: u rises, π falls (disinflation). This makes inflation procyclical with output in the short run.
Other Week-9 topics: S − I = NX identity (V21 A1); Okun's law sign T/F trap.

Week 10 — Solow growth model Mankiw Ch. 7 (repeat), 8, 9

Almost certain to be Problem Set 4 on your exam (≈20%).

The big question: why are some countries rich?

Two countries that look similar today can grow apart over time. Solow's model explains long-run growth using three ingredients: capital accumulation, population growth, and technology.

Setup (per worker form)

To make life simple we divide everything by the number of workers L:

  • y = Y/L = output per worker  (lower-case = per worker)
  • k = K/L = capital per worker
  • Production function: y = f(k), with diminishing returns (each extra unit of k adds less)

Standard exam form: y = k1/2 (square root). Sometimes with a scale factor like y = 3·k1/2.

The fundamental equation (with population growth n and tech growth g)

Δk = sf(k) − (δ + n + g) × k
Δk = change in capital per worker  |  s = saving rate (fraction of y saved)  |  f(k) = production function = √k typically  |  δ (delta) = depreciation rate (e.g. 3%)  |  n = population growth rate  |  g = technology growth rate

In words: capital per worker grows when saving per worker (s × f(k)) exceeds break-even investment (δ + n + g) × k. Break-even investment is what's needed to:

  • Replace depreciated capital (δk)
  • Equip new workers as the population grows (nk)
  • Maintain the capital ratio as technology improves and effective workers multiply (gk)
Plain-English picture. Imagine a treadmill. Saving s·f(k) pushes capital per worker upward. Depreciation, more workers, and tech growth all eat away at it. The economy reaches a "steady state" when the upward push exactly cancels the downward drag — you're walking on the treadmill at the same pace as it moves backward. That's k*.
The Solow diagram. Production y = f(k) (blue) is concave. Saving sf(k) (green) is a flatter version. Break-even investment (δ+n+g)k (red) is a straight line. Steady state k* is where sf(k) crosses (δ+n+g)k. Consumption per worker c* = y* − sy* is the gap between the blue and green curves at k*.

Steady state — the long-run equilibrium

At steady state, Δk = 0, so sf(k*) = (δ + n + g) × k*. With the square-root production function:

sk*1/2 = (δ + n + g)·k*
Divide both sides by k*1/2:
s = (δ + n + g)·k*1/2
k* = [s / (δ + n + g)]²
k* = steady-state capital per worker (the asterisk * means "at steady state")

Once you have k*:

  • y* = √k*  (steady-state output per worker)
  • Saving per worker = s·y*  (s = saving rate, y* = output per worker)
  • Consumption per worker c* = (1 − s)·y*  (c* = steady-state consumption per worker — this is what you want to maximise!)

What changes the steady state?

Changek*y*Long-run growth rate of y
Higher saving rate s↑↑Unchanged (only level, not growth)
Higher population growth n↓↓Unchanged
Higher depreciation δ↓↓Unchanged
Higher technology growth g↓ (in level form, per effective worker)per worker grows at g↑ (this is the only thing that drives long-run per-capita growth)
The key insight Solow taught us: capital alone can't sustain growth — it hits diminishing returns. Only technology improvements drive sustained growth in living standards.

The Golden Rule

The Golden Rule level of capital is the k that maximises consumption per worker in steady state. The condition:

MPK = δ + n + g (at the Golden Rule)
MPK = marginal product of capital (extra output from one more unit of K)  |  δ = depreciation  |  n = population growth  |  g = technology growth. So at the Golden Rule, the return on extra capital exactly equals what's needed to maintain it.
Golden Rule in plain English. Imagine you can choose any saving rate. If you save very little, your steady-state capital and output are small — not much to consume even though you're consuming a big share of a small pie. If you save a lot, capital is huge but you're consuming a tiny share — also not much. Somewhere in the middle is the saving rate that maximises your slice of consumption per worker. That's the Golden Rule. The mathematical condition for that point is MPK = δ + n + g.

Is the steady state always at the Golden Rule? NO. Steady state depends on the saving rate s. The Golden Rule is a property of preferences. Equality only happens for a specific s = sgold:

  • If s < sgold: under-saving. Raising s would (eventually) raise consumption.
  • If s > sgold: over-saving. Lowering s would raise consumption right away.
Why is the steady state usually NOT at the Golden Rule? Because households (or governments) choose s based on their own preferences, taxation, etc. — not based on solving an optimisation problem to maximise long-run consumption. Most countries empirically save less than the Golden Rule rate, because saving means giving up consumption today for higher consumption far in the future, and people heavily discount that distant gain.

Worked example (mirrors S25 PS4)

Country A: s = 0.15, n = 0.04, δ = 0.03, g = 0.02. So δ+n+g = 0.09.
k*A = (0.15/0.09)² ≈ 2.78
y*A = √2.78 ≈ 1.667
sy*A = 0.15 × 1.667 ≈ 0.250
c*A = 0.85 × 1.667 ≈ 1.417

Country B: s = 0.20, n = 0.04, δ = 0.03, g = 0.04. So δ+n+g = 0.11.
k*B = (0.20/0.11)² ≈ 3.31
y*B = √3.31 ≈ 1.818

Comparative statics. Higher saving rate s shifts sf(k) up → new steady state at k** (rightward, higher y*). Higher population growth n makes the break-even line steeper → new steady state at k'* (leftward, lower y*).

Week 11 — Economic fluctuations + IS-LM Mankiw Ch. 9 (repeat), 10, 11

Why do we need a different model for the short run?

The classical model assumes prices are flexible and markets always clear. In the short run, prices are sticky — wages are negotiated yearly, menu prices change slowly, contracts lock in nominal payments. So when demand drops, output falls (not just prices). This is what creates business cycles.

Two-speed economy: in the long run, prices adjust → classical model holds → output is at potential Y̅. In the short run, prices are sticky → output can be above or below Y̅ → IS-LM applies.

The IS-LM model — overview (Ch 10–11)

Two markets, two curves, two unknowns (Y and r).

  • IS curve: all (Y, r) combos where the goods market is in equilibrium. Slopes down.
  • LM curve: all (Y, r) combos where the money market is in equilibrium. Slopes up.
  • Equilibrium = where they cross.

Building the IS curve

Start with the goods-market equilibrium: Y = C(Y−T) + I(r) + G.

If r is low, investment is high, planned expenditure is high, equilibrium Y is high. If r is high, investment is low, Y is low. So lower r ↔ higher Y. This gives a downward-sloping curve in (Y, r) space.

What does the IS curve REALLY represent — in plain English. Think of every point on the IS curve as a possible "world" where the goods market is balanced. At each interest rate r, there's exactly one level of Y that makes planned spending = output. When r is low, investment is cheap, so spending is high, and Y is high to match. When r is high, investment is squeezed, spending is low, Y is low. The curve just connects all these (Y, r) "balance points." That's why it slopes down.

The Keynesian cross (the foundation)

Planned Expenditure (PE) = C + I + G
PE = total amount agents plan to spend  |  C = a + b·(Y−T) where b = MPC ∈ (0,1)
At equilibrium, PE = Y. Solving: Y* = (autonomous spending) / (1 − MPC)
"Autonomous spending" = the parts that don't depend on Y, i.e. a (constant in C) − b·T + I + G  |  MPC = marginal propensity to consume
The Keynesian cross. The 45° line shows where PE = Y. The PE line has slope MPC (less than 1) and y-intercept = autonomous spending. They cross at Y*. If government spending rises, PE shifts up parallel — Y* moves right.

Multipliers (memorise these — exam favourites)

MPC = marginal propensity to consume (out of one extra dollar of disposable income, how much is spent on C). Always between 0 and 1.

MultiplierFormulaWith MPC = 0.75What it means
Government spending (ΔY/ΔG)1 / (1 − MPC)4↑G of 100 raises Y by 400
Tax (ΔY/ΔT)−MPC / (1 − MPC)−3↑T of 100 lowers Y by 300
Balanced budget (ΔG = ΔT)11↑G and ↑T of 100 raises Y by 100 (net)
Why is the multiplier > 1? When G rises by 100, the government spends 100 → that's 100 of income for someone → they spend 75% of it (MPC = 0.75) → that's 75 of income for someone else → they spend 56.25 → … the rounds add up to 100 × 4 = 400.

Building the LM curve

Money market equilibrium: real money supply = real money demand. M/P = L(Y, r). Typical exam form: (M/P)d = Y − cr, set equal to the given M/P.

If Y rises, money demand rises. With M/P fixed, r must rise to clear the market. So higher Y ↔ higher r. Upward-sloping curve.

The LM curve in plain English. Same idea, but for the money market. Each point on the LM curve is a "world" where money supply = money demand. At low Y, people don't need much money for transactions, so the interest rate that clears the market is low. At high Y, transactions are heavy, money demand is high, and r must rise to get people to hold the existing fixed money supply. Connect those balance points — the curve slopes up.

Solving IS = LM

You write IS as Y = f(r), LM as Y = g(r), set them equal, solve for r*, plug back in for Y*. (See the worked exam Example 1 in your main study notes file.)

IS-LM equilibrium. IS slopes down (lower r → more I → more Y). LM slopes up (higher Y → more money demand → higher r). They cross at (Y*, r*).

Policy effects

PolicyCurveΔYΔrPlain-English

Fiscal contraction (↓G or ↑T)IS shifts left↓↓Mirror image.

Two key policy shocks. Fiscal expansion (left) shifts IS right → Y rises, r rises (crowding out). Monetary expansion (right) shifts LM right → Y rises, r falls.

Week 12 — IS-LM applications + Mundell-Fleming Mankiw Ch. 12–13

Applying IS-LM (Ch 12)

Once you have the basic IS-LM, you can analyze many real-world events: recessions, monetary policy, the Great Depression, etc.

Deriving aggregate demand (AD) from IS-LM

If P rises, real money supply M/P falls → LM shifts left → r rises → I falls → Y falls. So higher P → lower Y. That's the AD curve, which is downward-sloping in (P, Y) space.

Mundell-Fleming model (Ch 13) — open economy IS-LM

Same idea as IS-LM but in an open economy with perfect capital mobility (r = r*).

The two curves in (e, Y) space

  • IS*: Y = C(Y−T) + I(r*) + G + NX(e). Downward-sloping. Higher e → lower NX → lower Y.
  • LM*: M/P = L(Y, r*). With r* and M/P both fixed, this gives ONE specific Y. Vertical line.
Mundell-Fleming basic diagram. LM* is vertical because r is pinned at r* by perfect capital mobility — there's only one Y that clears the money market. IS* slopes down because higher e reduces NX, which reduces Y.

The four cases — memorize these

ER regimePolicyEffect on YEffect on eWhy
Floating↑G or ↓TNonee appreciatesIS* shifts right but LM* is vertical. Higher r-pressure attracts capital → e ↑ → NX ↓ → cancels boost. Fiscal is INEFFECTIVE.
↑M↑e depreciatesLM* shifts right. Capital flows out → e ↓ → NX ↑ → Y ↑. Monetary is EFFECTIVE.
Fixed↑G or ↓T↑e fixedTo prevent appreciation, central bank sells own currency → M ↑ → LM* shifts right alongside. Full effect. Fiscal is EFFECTIVE.
↑MNonee fixed↑M would depreciate; CB buys back currency, undoing ΔM. Monetary is INEFFECTIVE.
Memory hook: "Floating gives monetary, fixed gives fiscal." This one sentence is the heart of Mundell-Fleming. Memorize it.
Why is fiscal ineffective under floating? — the intuition that always confuses students.
Imagine the government boosts spending by 100. Normally that would raise Y. But here's what happens in a small open economy with floating rates:
1. Higher Y means higher money demand → upward pressure on r.
2. But r is supposed to equal r* (world rate). So foreign capital floods IN to capture the higher r.
3. Foreign investors must buy the domestic currency to invest here → currency APPRECIATES.
4. Currency appreciation makes our exports expensive abroad, our imports cheap → NET EXPORTS FALL.
5. The fall in NX exactly cancels the original boost from G.
Net result: Y unchanged, e appreciates. The fiscal stimulus was completely "leaked out" via the exchange rate channel.

Under FIXED rates, step 3 is blocked: the central bank prevents the appreciation by selling domestic currency, which expands M, shifts LM* right, and Y rises by the full amount. That's why fiscal is effective under fixed.

Trade policy (tariffs, quotas)

A tariff makes imports more expensive → ↓M (imports) → ↑NX (at any e) → IS* shifts right.

  • Under floating: e appreciates, Y unchanged (LM* still vertical). NX is the same in equilibrium (the appreciation cancels the tariff's effect on net exports).
  • Under fixed: Y rises (CB intervenes to hold e, M expands).
The four Mundell-Fleming cases. Memory hook: "Floating gives monetary, fixed gives fiscal." Floating+fiscal: IS* shifts but Y is pinned by vertical LM*; e appreciates. Floating+monetary: LM* shifts, Y rises, e depreciates. Fixed+fiscal: IS* shifts, CB expands M to defend the peg, LM* shifts alongside, Y rises. Fixed+monetary: CB must reverse ΔM to defend e — no effect on Y.

Week 13 — Aggregate Supply + Dynamic AD-AS + Consumption Mankiw Ch. 14–16

Aggregate Supply (Ch 14)

So far we've drawn AD without thinking carefully about supply. Now we add it.

Three models of short-run AS — same general result

Mankiw gives three theories (sticky-wage, sticky-price, imperfect-information). All boil down to the same thing:

Y = Y̅ + α(P − Pe) — output rises above potential when P > expected P

The three curves

CurveSlope in (P, Y)What it means
ADDownHigher P → lower real money → higher r → lower I, Y. Lower P → opposite.
SRASUpHigher P (above expected) → firms produce more.
LRASVertical at Y̅In the long run, P doesn't affect output. Output is fixed by K, L, A.

Demand shocks

↑G shifts AD right → SR: P↑, Y↑ above Y̅. Over time, Pe rises → SRAS shifts up → returns to Y̅ at higher P. Long run: same Y̅, higher P. Demand shocks affect output short-run, prices long-run.

Supply shocks (oil shock, COVID, war)

An adverse supply shock shifts SRAS up/left.

  • SR: Y↓, P↑ — both bad. This is stagflation.
  • LR: wages and Pe adjust → SRAS shifts back. Y returns to Y̅, but P stays elevated.
  • Policy options: accommodate (↑M shifts AD right, restores Y but worsens inflation) or wait (slow recovery, but P returns).
AD-AS with an adverse supply shock. SRAS shifts up from SRAS₁ to SRAS₂. The economy moves from point A (Y̅, P₁) to point B (Y₂ < Y̅, P₂ > P₁) — output falls AND prices rise simultaneously. This is stagflation. Long run, expectations adjust, SRAS drifts back, but the price level stays elevated permanently unless something offsets it.

Dynamic AD-AS (Ch 15) — bonus content

This is a more sophisticated model that combines the Phillips curve, monetary policy rule, and dynamic adjustments. Probably not on the exam in detail, but you should know:

  • Inflation expectations adjust over time (adaptive or rational).
  • Central banks set r based on a rule like the Taylor rule.
  • The economy converges to long-run equilibrium gradually.

Consumption theories (Ch 16)

Almost certainly on your exam (PS5 a, ≈20%).

Three theories — compare and contrast

TheoryEquationPlain-English
Keynes (1936) Absolute Income HypothesisC = a + b·Yd, with 0 < b < 1
C = consumption, a = autonomous (constant), b = MPC, Yd = disposable income = Y−T		Current disposable income drives consumption. Period.
Modigliani (1954) Life-Cycle Hypothesis (LCH)C = (W + R·Y)/T
W = wealth, R = remaining working years, Y = annual income, T (in this formula) = total remaining years of life — NOT taxes!		People smooth consumption over their whole life. Save during work years, spend savings in retirement.
Friedman (1957) Permanent Income Hypothesis (PIH)C = α·YP
α (alpha) = consumption fraction, YP = permanent income (long-run expected income)		Only permanent (long-run) income matters. Transitory shocks barely affect C — people save windfalls and borrow against expected windfalls.
Watch out — letter overload again. In Modigliani's LCH formula, "T" means total remaining years of life, NOT taxes. And in some textbooks, he uses different letters entirely. The key insight is the same regardless: lifetime smoothing.

The Kuznets puzzle (and how PIH/LCH solve it)

Empirical fact: APC (average propensity to consume) is roughly constant in long-run time series, but it FALLS as income rises in cross-section. Keynes alone can't explain this — his theory predicts APC always falls with income.

PIH/LCH solve it: in cross-section, higher-income households have a larger share of transitory income (lucky year, bonus), so APC of permanent income looks lower. In time series, growth is mostly permanent, so APC stays constant.

Implications for policy (e.g., tax rebates)

  • Keynes: a one-time tax rebate raises C immediately by MPC × rebate.
  • PIH/LCH: a one-time rebate is mostly transitory → most of it is saved, very small effect on C.

Empirically, evidence is mixed but tends to favour PIH for predictable, large changes.

Week 15 — Consumption (cont'd), Investment, Financial Crises Mankiw Ch. 16, 17, 22

More on Consumption (Ch 16 finished)

Modern theories of consumption emphasise:

  • Rational expectations: people use all available information about future income.
  • Borrowing constraints: if you can't borrow, current income matters more than PIH suggests.
  • Habit formation: people get used to consumption levels and adjust slowly.
  • Behavioural factors: mental accounting, self-control problems, etc.

The Theory of Investment (Ch 17)

Three types of investment

  1. Business fixed investment: firms buying machines, factories, equipment.
  2. Residential investment: new housing.
  3. Inventory investment: changes in stocks of unsold goods.

What determines business investment?

Firms compare the cost of capital (real interest r + depreciation δ) with the marginal product of capital (MPK):

  • If MPK > r + δ, build more capital (positive net investment).
  • If MPK < r + δ, capital is too high, let it depreciate.

Therefore: investment is a decreasing function of r. I = I(r). Higher r → less I.

Tobin's q (extra)

q = (market value of firm) / (replacement cost of capital)

If q > 1, building new capital is profitable → invest. If q < 1, don't. q-theory connects investment to stock market valuations.

Financial Crises (Ch 22 — newer editions)

What is a financial crisis?

A sharp disruption in financial intermediation — banks and credit markets stop functioning normally. Examples: Great Depression 1929, Asian crisis 1997, Global Financial Crisis 2007–08.

How they unfold (typical pattern)

  1. Asset price boom (housing, stocks) — often credit-driven.
  2. Boom turns to bust → asset prices crash.
  3. Banks lose money → reduce lending → credit crunch.
  4. Reduced credit → reduced investment, consumption → recession.
  5. Bank runs / panics → confidence collapses → deeper recession.

Policy responses during crises

  • Lender of last resort: central bank lends to solvent-but-illiquid banks.
  • Bailouts and recapitalisations: government injects capital into failing banks.
  • Aggressive monetary easing: cut r to zero (or use unconventional tools like QE).
  • Fiscal stimulus: ↑G to support demand when private sector collapses.

Beyond the basic I(r) story:

Money Supply, Money Demand, Banking (Ch 18)

How banks "create" money

The central bank creates the monetary base B = currency in circulation + reserves at the central bank. Commercial banks then expand this through fractional-reserve lending.

Example. You deposit 1,000 DKK at the bank. The bank holds 10% as reserves (100 DKK) and lends out 900 DKK. The borrower spends it; the recipient deposits it elsewhere. Now there's 1,000 + 900 = 1,900 DKK of deposits in the system from your original 1,000. The bank does this again with the new 900 → another 810 created. The full money multiplier expansion: 1,000 / 0.10 = 10,000 DKK of total deposits.

The money multiplier (simple version)

m = 1 / rr, where rr = reserve ratio
m = money multiplier (NOT the same as MPC or marginal propensity to import — letters get reused!)  |  rr = fraction of deposits banks must hold as reserves (e.g. 0.10 = 10%)  |  B = monetary base (cash + bank reserves at central bank)  |  M = total money supply

Money supply M = m × monetary base B. So if B = 1,000 and rr = 0.10, M = 10,000.

Tools of monetary policy

  • Open market operations: central bank buys/sells government bonds → changes B → changes M.
  • Reserve requirements: change rr → change m → change M.
  • The discount rate: rate at which CB lends to commercial banks.

Money demand

People hold money for transactions and as a store of value. Demand depends on Y (more activity → more money needed) and i (higher i → more attractive to hold bonds, less attractive to hold cash).

L(Y, i) — money demand. ↑Y → ↑demand. ↑i → ↓demand.
L() is just the symbol for "the money demand function." Y = real income, i = nominal interest rate. So money demand depends on income (positively — more transactions need more money) and the nominal rate (negatively — higher i makes bonds more attractive than cash).
The money market. Money supply M/P is fixed in the short run (vertical). Money demand slopes down (higher i means people prefer bonds over cash). Equilibrium gives interest rate i*. If Y rises, the demand curve shifts right and i must rise — this is exactly why the LM curve slopes up.
Why the LM curve slopes up — the simplest version. Think of the money market like a parking lot. Money supply is the number of parking spaces, fixed. Money demand is how many cars want to park. When the economy is busy (high Y), more cars need parking. With fixed spaces, the parking fee (i) must rise to keep things balanced. So higher Y means higher i. Plot that relationship and it slopes up. That's LM.
Exam check (Week 16) — Past exam topics:
Concept question (V22 PS5): "Explain how short-term interest rate changes will influence national output Y and investment I."
Model answer: "An increase in the short-term interest rate raises the cost of borrowing for firms. By the investment function I = I(r), higher r reduces I directly. Lower I reduces aggregate demand: AD shifts left in (P, Y) space, lowering Y. There's a secondary effect: lower Y reduces disposable income, lowering C as well, amplifying the contraction (multiplier effect, weakened by the higher r through crowding-out logic). The net effect: higher r → lower I → lower Y. This is the transmission mechanism of monetary policy."
Money creation by banks is rarely tested directly but provides intuition for why central banks have leverage over M/P.

Week 17 — Government Debt + Stabilization Policy Mankiw Ch. 19, 21

Government Debt (Ch 19)

Definitions

  • Budget deficit = G − T (in any one year, spending > revenue).
  • Government debt = sum of past deficits (the running total).
  • Debt-to-GDP ratio = debt / nominal GDP. The relevant indicator for sustainability.

Two views of debt

Traditional view: Higher G → larger deficit → less national saving → higher r → less I → lower long-run output. Future generations are worse off because the capital stock is smaller.

Ricardian equivalence (Barro): Forward-looking households see today's deficit as future taxes, save more today to pay future taxes, so private saving rises by exactly the amount public saving falls. National saving stays the same. The view that debt-financed tax cuts have no effect.

Empirically, Ricardian equivalence holds only partially. Most economists believe the traditional view: deficits do reduce national saving and crowd out investment, but maybe not 1-for-1.

When is debt sustainable?

If interest rate r is below the growth rate g, debt-to-GDP can be stable even with persistent deficits. This is the famous "r < g" condition. Recent decades have seen r < g in most rich countries, which is why Denmark and the EU can run debt without explosion.

Stabilization Policy (Ch 21)

Should the government try to smooth out business cycles?

Two camps:

ViewRecommendsWhy
Active stabilization (Keynesian)Use fiscal/monetary policy to dampen cycles.Recessions are costly (unemployment, lost output). We have tools — use them.
Passive policy (Classical / monetarist)Let markets work; follow simple rules.Active policy creates lags, mistakes, political distortions. Often makes things worse.

Why active policy is hard — three reasons

  1. Recognition lag: data on the economy comes with a delay; you don't know you're in a recession until months later.
  2. Implementation lag: passing fiscal legislation takes time; even monetary policy decisions take a few weeks.
  3. Effectiveness lag: once policy is in place, it takes 6–18 months to work through the economy.

By the time your stimulus hits, the recession may be over and you're now over-stimulating a recovery → causing inflation.

Rules vs Discretion

Discretion: central bank decides each meeting based on judgement.

Rule: follows a formula like the Taylor rule: r = r* + 0.5(π − π*) + 0.5(Y − Y̅)/Y̅. Reduces political pressure and time-inconsistency problems but loses flexibility.

Time inconsistency (Kydland-Prescott)

Central bank announces "we'll keep inflation low" → people believe → set wages low. Then CB has incentive to surprise everyone with higher inflation to boost output. People learn this pattern → expect higher inflation. So discretionary policy ends up with high inflation AND no output gain. Solution: commitment to a rule, or independent central banks.

Exam check (Week 17) — Past exam question + worked solution:

From S25 PS1h (the "in your opinion" question): "The government implements a contractionary fiscal policy by raising T from 150 to 300. Explain how this policy will affect society as a whole."
Model answer (anchored in theory, not just opinion):
"A higher tax rate reduces disposable income, which by the consumption function lowers C. Lower C reduces aggregate demand, lowering Y (in our IS-LM example, from 1,550 to ~1,497). This is the short-run cost: lower output likely means lower employment, hurting workers and households in the immediate term.

However, there are offsetting benefits. First, the lower r (from 3% to 2.34%) reduces crowding out of private investment — over time, more I means more capital accumulation and higher long-run Y. Second, higher tax revenue with G unchanged means a smaller deficit, slowing the accumulation of public debt. This supports long-run fiscal sustainability and avoids the costs of debt service.

The policy involves a classic trade-off: short-run pain (lower Y, higher unemployment) for long-run gain (less debt, more I, healthier public finances). Whether it's wise depends on the state of the economy. If the economy is below potential, the contractionary effect deepens the recession — bad timing. If the economy is overheating with high inflation, contractionary fiscal makes sense. So context matters."
Other Week-17 topics: Ricardian equivalence (forward-looking households offset government deficits with private saving — useful background for "in your opinion" questions). Time-inconsistency / rules vs discretion explains why central banks are independent.

Big-Picture Summary — How Everything Fits Together

If you only had 30 minutes to revise, this is what to remember:

The two-speed economy

  • Long run (classical): Y is set by K, L, A (production function). Money is neutral. Prices adjust. Solow growth model. Loanable funds market clears.
  • Short run (Keynesian): Prices are sticky. AD shifts cause Y to deviate from Y̅. IS-LM applies. Monetary and fiscal policy matter.

The four "what shifts what" diagrams (memorise visually)

  • ↑G → IS shifts right → Y↑, r↑ (closed); under floating ER, Y stays the same and e appreciates.
  • ↑M → LM shifts right → Y↑, r↓ (closed); under fixed ER, e is held but ΔM gets undone.
  • Adverse supply shock → SRAS shifts up → Y↓, P↑ (stagflation).
  • ↑s in Solow → k* rises along the saving curve, y* rises but long-run growth rate is unchanged.

The five families of formulas (with every letter explained)

  1. National accounts: Y = C + I + G (+NX); S − I = NX; multiplier = 1/(1−MPC).
    Y = output, C = consumption, I = investment, G = government spending, NX = net exports (X − IM), S = saving, MPC = marginal propensity to consume.
  2. Money: M·V = P·Y; i = r + πe; M/P = L(Y, i).
    M = money supply, V = velocity of money, P = price level, i = nominal interest, r = real interest, πe = expected inflation, L() = money demand function.
  3. Production: Y = A·Kα·L(1−α); MPL = (1−α)Y/L; MPK = αY/K.
    A = technology, K = capital, L = labour, α = capital share, 1−α = labour share, MPL = marginal product of labour, MPK = marginal product of capital.
  4. Solow: Δk = sf(k) − (δ+n+g)k; k* = (s/(δ+n+g))²; golden rule MPK = δ+n+g.
    k = capital per worker, s = saving rate, δ = depreciation rate, n = population growth rate, g = technology growth rate, k* = steady-state k.
  5. Phillips/Okun: π = πe − β(u−un) + ν; ΔY/Y ≈ −β(u−un).
    π = inflation, πe = expected inflation, β = sensitivity coefficient, u = unemployment rate, un = natural rate of unemployment, ν = supply shock.
